Quick Reference

The passé simple is the literary equivalent of the passé composé. It describes completed past actions and is used almost exclusively in:

  • Formal written French (literature, history, journalism)
  • Third person (il/elle/on, ils/elles) most commonly
  • Narrative fiction and historical texts

⚠️ You will rarely USE this tense in speaking or informal writing, but you MUST be able to recognize and understand it.

Quick Reference — Passé Simple vs. Imparfait in Literature

In literary texts, the passé simple plays the SAME role as the passé composé in spoken French:

Literary WrittenSpoken/Informal Written
Passé simple + ImparfaitPassé composé + Imparfait
Il faisait beau quand il arriva.Il faisait beau quand il est arrivé.

The SAME rules for choosing between "completed event" and "background/ongoing/habitual" apply — the only difference is that passé simple replaces passé composé.

Answers vary. Sample:

Literary version (PS + IMP):

Marie était assise dans le salon. Le feu crépitait doucement dans la cheminée et la pluie battait contre les fenêtres. Soudain, le téléphone sonna. Elle décrocha et écouta en silence. Spoken version (PC + IMP):

Marie était assise dans le salon. Le feu crépitait doucement dans la cheminée et la pluie battait contre les fenêtres. Soudain, le téléphone a sonné. Elle a décroché et elle a écouté en silence. Comparison:
The passé simple version feels more literary, more distant, and more dramatic — as if told by an omniscient narrator. The rhythm is tighter and more elegant. The passé composé version feels more intimate, more personal, and closer to the reader's experience — as if someone is telling you the story directly. The PS creates aesthetic distance; the PC creates emotional proximity.

Exercise D — Reading Comprehension

  1. "Faisait" is imparfait — used for weather description / background setting.

  2. "Aperçut" is passé simple — it expresses a completed, sudden action (he noticed/spotted something) that advances the narrative.

  3. "Avait perdu" is plus-que-parfait — it expresses an action (losing the wallet) that occurred before the announcement; it's also in reported context (on avait perdu = someone had lost).

  4. Passé simple verbs: aperçut, se baissa, ramassa, se mit, vit, parut, dit, oublia, retentit, fit, déclara, protesta, crut, fut accusé (passive PS).

  5. Imparfait verbs: faisait, se couchait, doraient, venait (de), détestait, était (ce n'était qu').

  6. Plus-que-parfait verbs: avait perdu, avait vu.

  7. "Déclara" is PS — the main narrative event (Malandain's declaration). "Avait vu" is PQP — it refers to the earlier event of seeing, which happened before the declaration. "Ramasser" is an infinitive complement — what he claimed to have seen Hauchecorne do.

Key Takeaways for Passé Simple:

  1. Recognition > Production — You need to READ it, not necessarily write it in conversation
  2. PS = PC in function — Both express completed past actions; PS is literary, PC is spoken
  3. PS + IMP = the literary narrative system — PS for events, IMP for background
  4. Three irregular patterns — -is (voir, prendre, faire...), -us (avoir, pouvoir, savoir...), -int (venir, tenir...)
  5. Watch for ambiguous forms — il finit, il dit, il vit can be present OR passé simple
  6. Third person dominates — il/elle and ils/elles are the forms you'll encounter most
