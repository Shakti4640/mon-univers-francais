Quick Reference

The passé composé describes a completed action in the past. It is formed with:

SUBJECT + AUXILIARY (avoir or être) in present tense + PAST PARTICIPLE

Past Participle Formation:

Verb TypeRuleExample
-ER verbs-éparler → parlé
-IR verbs-ifinir → fini
-RE verbs-uvendre → vendu

AVOIR as auxiliary (most verbs):

Subjectavoir+ past participle
j'aiparlé
tuasparlé
il/elle/onaparlé
nousavonsparlé
vousavezparlé
ils/ellesontparlé

Quick Reference — Reflexive Verbs in Passé Composé

ALL reflexive verbs use ÊTRE as their auxiliary in the passé composé.

Structure: Subject + reflexive pronoun + ÊTRE + past participle

SubjectExample: se lever
jeme suis levé(e)
tut'es levé(e)
ils'est levé
elles'est levée
ons'est levé(s)
nousnous sommes levé(e)s
vousvous êtes levé(e)(s)
ilsse sont levés
ellesse sont levées

⚠️ Agreement Rule for Reflexives:
The past participle agrees with the reflexive pronoun (which represents the subject) ONLY when the reflexive pronoun is a direct object. If the verb has a separate direct object after it, there is NO agreement.

AgreementNo Agreement
Elle s'est lavée. (She washed herself)Elle s'est lavé les mains. (She washed her hands)
Ils se sont regardés. (They looked at each other)Ils se sont parlé. (They spoke to each other — parler à)

sortirNous sommes sortis.Nous avons sorti le chien.
rentrerIls sont rentrés.Ils ont rentré la voiture.
retournerElle est retournée en France.Elle a retourné la crêpe.
passerIl est passé devant la maison.Il a passé un examen.

Exercise B — Complex Agreement

  1. fait construire — No agreement on "fait" when followed by an infinitive (faire + infinitive = invariable per 1990 reform / traditional rule: the subject of "construire" is not "maison"). "Fait" before infinitive is invariable.
  2. fallu — No agreement. "Falloir" is impersonal; "qu'" refers to "efforts" but "efforts" is not the direct object of "falloir" — the clause "faire" is.
  3. laissé — "Laisser" + infinitive: under the 1990 spelling reform, "laissé" before an infinitive is invariable (like "fait"). Traditionally, some argue "laissée" since "elle" is the one falling. Either is defensible; modern standard = laissé.
  4. entendu — No agreement (modern rule). "Les chansons" is the object of "chanter," not of "entendre." Under traditional rules, some would write "entendues" if the songs are the subject of the singing. Modern standard: invariable before infinitive = entendu.
  5. succédé — No agreement. "Succéder à quelqu'un" — "se" is indirect object.
  6. vu — Same logic as #4. "La pièce" is the object of "jouer," not of "voir." Modern standard: vu (invariable before infinitive). Traditional: vue is also accepted.
  7. dormi — No agreement. "Trois heures" is an adverbial expression of duration, not a direct object. (You sleep FOR three hours, not you sleep three hours as a direct object.)
  8. plu — No agreement. "Plaire à quelqu'un" — "se" is indirect object.
  9. laissées — Here, "les pommes" (= que) are doing the action of "pourrir" (rotting), so agreement is made with the preceding DO. Under 1990 reform, "laissé" before infinitive could be invariable. Traditional grammar: laissées (the apples are the ones rotting).
  10. fait — No agreement. "Fait" + infinitive is always invariable. She had her hair cut (by someone else).

Exercise I — Subtle Distinctions

1a & 1b: Both translate to "Elle est partie."
Explanation: English distinguishes between "she left" (simple past, narrative) and "she has left" (present perfect, result still relevant). French uses the passé composé for BOTH. Context determines the nuance. In spoken French, the passé composé has absorbed both functions.

2a & 2b: Both translate to "J'ai mangé."
Explanation: Same principle. "I ate" and "I have eaten" are both rendered by the passé composé. To emphasize present relevance, French might add context: "J'ai déjà mangé" (= I've already eaten, so I'm not hungry).

3a & 3b: Both translate to "Il a vécu à Paris." (or "Il a habité à Paris.")
Explanation: Both the completed-and-over meaning and the life-experience meaning use the passé composé. French may add context for clarity: "Il a vécu à Paris pendant cinq ans" (completed) vs. "Il a déjà vécu à Paris" (experience).

4a: "Ils ne se sont pas parlé depuis la dispute." (passé composé — negative + depuis)
4b: "Ils ne se parlent pas depuis la dispute." (présent — ongoing state)
Explanation: With "depuis" + negative, French can use either the passé composé (emphasizing the action that stopped) or the présent (emphasizing the ongoing state of not speaking). The passé composé is more common and natural here.
