🔵 WHAT COULD BE DONE

Option A: Write Without Planning (Free Writing)

  • → No pipeline → Random tool deployment → Inconsistent quality
  • → Some sections strong, others weak → Unbalanced scoring
  • → Time wasted on restructuring mid-writing
  • → NOT recommended

Option B: Plan Excessively (10+ Minutes Planning)

  • → Perfect plan but insufficient writing time
  • → Incomplete response → Dimension 1 penalty (word count)
  • → Plan quality does not compensate for missing text
  • → NOT recommended

Option C: Follow the 8-Step Pipeline With Balanced Time Allocation

  • → Each step feeds the next → No wasted effort
  • → Planning is focused (5–8 minutes) → Writing is efficient (18–22 minutes) → Review catches errors (3–5 minutes)
  • → THIS IS THE CORRECT APPROACH

Option D: Memorize a Template Response and Adapt It

  • → Risky → Template may not fit the prompt
  • → Evaluators recognize templates → Authenticity penalty

❌ WHAT SHOULD NOT DO

1. Do NOT Try to Use EVERY Tool From EVERY Chapter in One Response

REALITY CHECK:
  You have learned tools from 19 chapters
  A 220-word response has approximately 12–15 sentences
  You CANNOT demonstrate every tool in 12 sentences

WHAT TO AIM FOR:
  → 1 clear causal chain (Chapter 15) → 3–4 sentences
  → 1 clear concession (Chapter 16) → 2 sentences
  → 4–5 different sentence types (Chapter 17) → across entire response
  → 8–12 B2 vocabulary items (Chapter 18) → distributed throughout
  → 5–6 varied cohesion tools (Chapter 19) → at sentence transitions
  → 1 introduction formula (Chapter 10) → 3 sentences
  → 1 conclusion formula (Chapter 11) → 2–3 sentences

THIS IS ENOUGH for a strong B2 score
Trying to add MORE → Overloads the text → Quality drops

2. Do NOT Write the Introduction Last

  • Some candidates write the body first, then the introduction
  • Problem 1 → Your introduction sets the THESIS that the body must support
    • → Without writing it first, your body may DRIFT from your intended position
  • Problem 2 → If you run out of time, a missing introduction costs MORE than a missing conclusion
    • → Introduction = first impression = Dimension 1 + Dimension 2 foundation
  • ALWAYS write in order: Introduction → Body 1 → Body 2 → Conclusion

3. Do NOT Rewrite Sentences During the Exam

  • If a sentence is imperfect but UNDERSTANDABLE → Move on
  • Crossing out and rewriting wastes time AND creates messy presentation
  • The 80/20 rule → 80% of your score comes from having ALL sections complete with ADEQUATE quality
  • The remaining 20% comes from polish
  • Complete and adequate > Incomplete and polished

4. Do NOT Copy Phrases Directly From the Prompt

❌ COPYING:
  Prompt: "...de plus en plus de personnes préfèrent faire leurs achats en ligne..."
  Your introduction: "De plus en plus de personnes préfèrent faire leurs achats en ligne."
  → Word-for-word copy → Shows no reformulation ability → Dimension 3 penalty

✅ REFORMULATING:
  Prompt: "...de plus en plus de personnes préfèrent faire leurs achats en ligne..."
  Your introduction: "Le commerce en ligne connaît une croissance sans précédent."
  → Same idea → Different words → Demonstrates lexical competence → Dimension 3 ↑

5. Do NOT Neglect Paragraph Separation

  • Each section (Introduction, Body 1, Body 2, Conclusion) MUST be a separate paragraph
  • Visual paragraph breaks signal STRUCTURE to the evaluator
  • A single block of text with no paragraph breaks → Dimension 2 penalty
  • Even if your cohesion is perfect, VISUAL organization matters

6. Do NOT Write More Than 280 Words

  • Beyond 280 words:
    • → More opportunities for errors → Dimension 4 risk increases
    • → Time pressure for review → Errors go uncaught
    • → Evaluator fatigue → Negative impression
    • → No extra credit for length
  • The sweet spot is 210–250 words
  • If you find yourself at 270+, you are being VERBOSE, not thorough
  • Cut, do not add

How the Evaluator Reads Your Complete Response

FIRST READ (30 seconds — General Impression):
  → "Does this LOOK like a structured response?"
  → Paragraph breaks visible? ✅ → Good first impression
  → Length appropriate? ✅ → Good first impression

🕳️ COMMON PITFALLS AND FAILURE MODES

Pitfall 1: The "Perfect Introduction, Empty Body" Syndrome

  • Spending too long perfecting the introduction → Running out of time for the body
  • Detection → If your introduction takes more than 5 minutes → STOP and move to the body
  • Rule → The body is worth MORE than the introduction → Allocate time accordingly

Pitfall 2: The "Two Thèses" Error

  • Writing TWO paragraphs that BOTH argue FOR your position → No antithèse
  • → Evaluator sees: "No nuance, no opposing view considered"
  • → Dimension 2 penalized for lack of dialectical structure
  • Detection → Before writing Body 2, ask: "Am I about to argue for the OTHER side?"

Pitfall 3: The "Conclusion That Contradicts the Body" Error

  • Body argues AGAINST online shopping → Conclusion says "Online shopping is great"
  • → Evaluator sees: "This candidate is confused about their own position"
  • → Dimension 2 catastrophe
  • Detection → Before writing the conclusion, re-read your thesis statement → Does your conclusion ALIGN?

Pitfall 4: The "Missing Example" Error

  • Arguments without examples → Abstract and unconvincing
  • The prompt SPECIFICALLY asks for examples ("à l'aide d'exemples")
  • → Missing examples = Dimension 1 penalty (did not follow instructions)
  • Rule → At least ONE concrete example in the body (minimum)
  • → Two examples (one per body paragraph) is ideal

Pitfall 5: The "Tool Showcase" Error

  • Forcing every possible tool into the response regardless of whether it fits
  • → Concession where none is needed → Inversion that sounds awkward → Vocabulary that does not fit the theme
  • Rule → Tools serve the ARGUMENT → If a tool does not strengthen the argument, do not use it
  • → Quality of tool deployment > Quantity of tools deployed

Pitfall 6: The "No Review" Error

  • Writing until the last second → No time to re-read
  • → Preventable errors remain: missing agreements, wrong connector, incomplete sentence
  • Rule → STOP writing with 3–5 minutes remaining → Use that time for review
  • → Refer to Chapter 26 (time management) for detailed time allocation strategy
