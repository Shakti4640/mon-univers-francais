📘 CHAPTER 8

FOR AND AGAINST: LEARNING TO SEE BOTH SIDES BEFORE CHOOSING ONE

🔴 HYPOTHETICAL PROBLEM

You are a TEF candidate named Ravi
He completed Phase 1 (Chapters 1–6) → He can assemble complete responses
He studied Chapter 7 → He has a full connector toolkit across 5 functions
He encounters this practice prompt:

"Certains pays ont décidé d'interdire les sacs en plastique. Pensez-vous que cette interdiction soit une bonne mesure? Justifiez votre réponse à l'aide d'arguments et d'exemples."

Ravi applies his skills:

  • Chapter 2 extraction: Type 6 (Policy/Action) — "Is banning plastic bags a good measure?"
  • Chapter 3 opinion: He decides YES — he strongly supports the ban
  • Chapter 4 justification: Two reasons — environmental protection + behavioral change
  • Chapter 5 structure: Introduction → Body (2 units) → Conclusion
  • Chapter 7 connectors: Sequence + Cause + Consequence + Illustration

His response:

"De nos jours, la pollution plastique constitue un défi environnemental majeur. Je suis fermement convaincu que l'interdiction des sacs en plastique représente une mesure indispensable.

Tout d'abord, cette interdiction contribuerait à la protection de l'environnement, car les sacs en plastique mettent plusieurs centaines d'années à se décomposer. Par conséquent, leur élimination réduirait considérablement la pollution des océans et des sols.

Par ailleurs, cette mesure encouragerait un changement de comportement chez les consommateurs. En effet, l'obligation d'utiliser des alternatives réutilisables sensibiliserait la population aux enjeux écologiques. Ainsi, les habitudes de consommation évolueraient progressivement vers un mode de vie plus durable.

En définitive, l'interdiction des sacs en plastique constitue une avancée écologique nécessaire qui bénéficierait à l'ensemble de la société."

Word count: ~140 words
Structure: Excellent ✅
Connectors: Varied and well-placed ✅
Grammar: Strong ✅

But the evaluator notes: "Argumentation unilatérale — absence de prise en compte des objections — réflexion insuffisamment nuancée pour le niveau B2"
Dimension 2 score: MODERATE (not high)
The evaluator wanted to see AWARENESS of the other side

❓ THE CORE QUESTION

  • Ravi wrote a technically excellent response — so why was it scored as only moderate?
  • What does "argumentation unilatérale" (one-sided argumentation) mean and why is it penalized?
  • How does seeing BOTH sides improve your score even when you clearly support ONE side?
  • How do you present the "against" without WEAKENING your "for" position?
  • When is one-sided argumentation acceptable, and when does it hurt you?

🟡 HOW TO APPROACH IT

Step 1: Understand the French Argumentative Culture of "Nuance"

FRENCH INTELLECTUAL TRADITION:
│
├── In French academic and argumentative culture, the HIGHEST value is NUANCE
├── Nuance = Showing that you understand COMPLEXITY
├── Complexity = Recognizing that most issues have MULTIPLE valid perspectives
│
├── A one-sided argument says: "I see only MY side"
│   → The evaluator reads: "This candidate thinks in black and white"
│   → Intellectual maturity assessment: LIMITED
│
├── A two-sided argument that CHOOSES one side says: "I see BOTH sides but I believe THIS"
│   → The evaluator reads: "This candidate thinks with depth and makes informed choices"
│   → Intellectual maturity assessment: STRONG
│
└── KEY INSIGHT: Acknowledging the other side does NOT weaken your position
    It STRENGTHENS it — because it shows you chose your position DESPITE knowing the objections
    A position held in AWARENESS of counterarguments is more credible than one held in IGNORANCE of them

Step 2: Understand the Difference Between "One-Sided" and "Two-Sided" Argumentation

ONE-SIDED ARGUMENTATION (Ravi's approach):
│
├── Structure:
│   ├── Introduction: "I believe X"
│   ├── Argument 1: "X is good because A"
│   ├── Argument 2: "X is good because B"
│   └── Conclusion: "X is good"
│
├── Every element pushes in the SAME direction
├── No acknowledgment that anyone could reasonably disagree
├── Feels ASSERTIVE but also INCOMPLETE
└── Score ceiling: B1–low B2 (adequate but not sophisticated)

TWO-SIDED ARGUMENTATION (Target approach):
│
├── Structure:
│   ├── Introduction: "I believe X"
│   ├── Argument 1: "X is good because A"
│   ├── Argument 2: "X is good because B"
│   ├── Acknowledgment: "Some argue Y against X" (brief)
│   ├── Rebuttal/Concession: "While Y has merit, X remains stronger because..."
│   └── Conclusion: "X is good, with awareness of its limitations"
│
├── Primary direction is still clearly FOR X
├── But a MOMENT of acknowledgment shows intellectual maturity
├── Feels CONFIDENT and THOUGHTFUL
└── Score ceiling: B2–C1 (sophisticated and nuanced)

Step 3: Understand the 3 Levels of "Seeing Both Sides"

LEVEL 1: SIMPLE ACKNOWLEDGMENT (easiest — start here)
├── Briefly mention that an opposing view EXISTS
├── Do NOT develop the opposing argument
├── Immediately return to your position
├── Typical location: Conclusion (ouverture) or brief sentence in body
├── Word cost: 10–20 words
└── Example: "Certes, certains pourraient s'opposer à cette mesure,
              mais ses bénéfices environnementaux sont indéniables."

LEVEL 2: CONCESSION (moderate — Chapter 16 will master this)
├── Acknowledge the opposing view AND admit it has PARTIAL validity
├── Then explain WHY your position is still stronger
├── Typical location: Start of Argument 2 or transition between arguments
├── Word cost: 20–40 words
└── Example: "Certes, l'interdiction des sacs en plastique peut sembler
              contraignante pour les commerçants. Cependant, les bénéfices
              à long terme pour l'environnement justifient largement
              cet inconvénient temporaire."

LEVEL 3: FULL THÈSE-ANTITHÈSE (advanced — Chapter 14 will master this)
├── Dedicate a full paragraph to the opposing view
├── Dedicate another full paragraph to your view
├── Conclude by choosing your side with reasoned justification
├── Typical location: Body structured as two opposing paragraphs
├── Word cost: 60–100 words (full paragraph for opposing side)
└── This is the most sophisticated approach — reserved for Chapters 14 and 21

Step 4: Understand WHERE in the Response to Place the "Other Side"

PLACEMENT OPTIONS:
│
├── OPTION A: In the CONCLUSION (Ouverture slot from Chapter 5)
│   ├── Easiest placement
│   ├── Does not disrupt body argument flow
│   ├── Adds nuance at the very end
│   ├── Word cost: Minimal (10–20 words)
│   └── Best for: Mini-responses and responses where body space is tight
│
├── OPTION B: As a BRIEF CONCESSION within the body
│   ├── Usually at the START of Argument 2 (before developing your second supporting point)
│   ├── Pattern: "Certes [opposing point], cependant [your rebuttal/second argument]"
│   ├── Word cost: Moderate (20–40 words)
│   └── Best for: Full responses (200–250 words) where you want to show nuance within the argument
│
├── OPTION C: As a SEPARATE PARAGRAPH between Argument 1 and Argument 2
│   ├── A mini-paragraph acknowledging the opposing view
│   ├── Followed by your rebuttal paragraph
│   ├── Word cost: Higher (40–60 words)
│   └── Best for: Extended responses or when the prompt explicitly asks for both sides
│
└── OPTION D: As a FULL THÈSE-ANTITHÈSE structure (Chapter 14)
    ├── Entire body restructured into FOR and AGAINST paragraphs
    ├── Word cost: High (full paragraph dedicated to opposing side)
    └── Best for: Type 2 prompts (Advantages/Disadvantages) or C1-level responses

🟢 HOW CAN WE SOLVE IT

The Solution = Mastering the "Bilateral Thinking" Process BEFORE Writing

  • Most candidates go one-sided because they decide their position and STOP THINKING
  • They think: "I am FOR this → Now I only need to find reasons FOR"
  • Bilateral thinking means: "I am FOR this → But what would someone AGAINST say? → How would I respond to them?"

🔹 THE BILATERAL THINKING PROTOCOL (Pre-Writing Mental Exercise)

STEP 1: DECIDE your position (Chapter 3 skill)
├── "I am FOR / AGAINST / NUANCED on this topic"
└── This does NOT change — your position is firm

STEP 2: BRAINSTORM arguments FOR your position (Chapter 4 skill)
├── List 3–4 potential reasons supporting your view
├── Select the 2 strongest for your response
└── These become your body arguments

STEP 3: BRAINSTORM arguments AGAINST your position (NEW SKILL)
├── Ask yourself: "If I were arguing the OTHER side, what would I say?"
├── List 2–3 potential counterarguments
├── You do NOT need to agree with them
├── You need to UNDERSTAND them
└── Select the 1 strongest counterargument

STEP 4: PREPARE your response to the counterargument
├── Ask: "Why is my position STILL stronger despite this counterargument?"
├── This becomes your REBUTTAL or CONCESSION
└── This is what makes your argument NUANCED

STEP 5: DECIDE where to place the counterargument acknowledgment
├── Option A: Conclusion ouverture (easiest)
├── Option B: Brief concession in body (moderate)
├── Option C: Separate paragraph (advanced)
└── Choose based on word count budget and confidence level

Applying the Bilateral Thinking Protocol to Ravi's prompt:

STEP 1: Position = FOR the ban on plastic bags

STEP 2: Arguments FOR:
├── Environmental protection (decomposition takes centuries)
├── Behavioral change (encourages sustainable habits)
├── Ocean wildlife protection (animals ingest plastic)
└── SELECTED: Environmental protection + Behavioral change (Ravi's original 2)

STEP 3: Arguments AGAINST:
├── Inconvenience for consumers (must remember reusable bags)
├── Economic impact on plastic bag manufacturers (job losses)
├── Alternatives may also have environmental costs (cotton bags require more resources)
└── SELECTED: Economic impact on manufacturers (strongest counterargument)

STEP 4: Rebuttal:
├── "While some jobs in plastic manufacturing may be affected,
│    new jobs in sustainable alternatives will emerge"
└── OR: "The short-term economic adjustment is justified by
         the long-term environmental benefits"

STEP 5: Placement:
├── Option B chosen: Brief concession at the start of Argument 2
└── Pattern: "Certes, cette mesure pourrait affecter certains secteurs
              économiques. Cependant, ..."

🔹 THE 4 PATTERNS FOR INTEGRATING THE "OTHER SIDE"

PATTERN 1: The Conclusion Nuance (Easiest — Level 1)

Where: Slot Y (Ouverture) of the conclusion from Chapter 5
Word cost: 10–20 words
Structure:

[Your synthesis — Slot X]
[Opposition acknowledgment — Slot Y using opposition connector from Chapter 7]

Example applied to Ravi's response — ONLY the conclusion changes:

"En définitive, l'interdiction des sacs en plastique constitue une avancée écologique nécessaire. Toutefois, il conviendrait d'accompagner cette mesure de solutions pour les secteurs économiques affectés."

Why this works:

  • → The synthesis (Slot X) FIRMLY restates Ravi's position
  • → The ouverture (Slot Y) acknowledges a counterargument WITHOUT undermining the position
  • → "Toutefois" (opposition connector from Chapter 7) signals the nuance
  • → "Il conviendrait de" (conditional) softens the acknowledgment
  • → Total cost: ~20 words added → Minimal impact on word budget
  • → Maximum impact on perceived nuance

PATTERN 2: The Concession Bridge (Moderate — Level 2)

Where: Between Argument 1 and Argument 2 in the body
Word cost: 20–40 words
Structure:

[Argument 1 — fully developed]
[Concession sentence: "Certes... cependant/néanmoins..."]
[Argument 2 — fully developed, often as the rebuttal]

Example applied to Ravi's response — a concession bridge is inserted:

"...Par conséquent, leur élimination réduirait considérablement la pollution des océans et des sols.

Certes, certains pourraient faire valoir que cette interdiction pénaliserait les fabricants de sacs en plastique et entraînerait des pertes d'emplois. Néanmoins, cette transition créerait simultanément de nouvelles opportunités dans le secteur des emballages durables. De surcroît, cette mesure encouragerait un changement de comportement chez les consommateurs..."

Why this works:

  • → "Certes" signals: "I am about to acknowledge the other side"
  • → The opposing argument is stated CLEARLY but BRIEFLY (one sentence)
  • → "Néanmoins" signals: "Despite that, here is why my position holds"
  • → The rebuttal flows naturally INTO Argument 2
  • → The evaluator sees: bilateral thinking + smooth argumentation flow
  • → Total cost: ~30 words → Manageable within 200–250 word budget

PATTERN 3: The Embedded Concession (Compact — Level 2)

Where: WITHIN a sentence, using a concessive structure
Word cost: 10–15 words (integrated into an existing sentence)
Structure:

"Bien que [opposing point], [your position remains]"
"Même si [opposing point], [your argument]"
"Malgré [opposing noun], [your position]"

Example applied within Ravi's body:

"Même si cette interdiction peut sembler contraignante à court terme, elle constitue un levier puissant pour encourager un changement durable des habitudes de consommation."

Why this works:

  • → The opposing point ("contraignante à court terme") is EMBEDDED within the sentence
  • → It does NOT get its own sentence or paragraph → Minimal disruption
  • → The main clause IMMEDIATELY counters it → Your position dominates
  • → Grammatically sophisticated (triggers concessive structures → Dimension 4 boost)
  • → Total cost: ~10 extra words woven into an existing sentence
  • → Very efficient use of word budget

Grammar note for embedded concessions:

"Bien que" + SUBJUNCTIVE
├── ✅ "Bien que cette mesure puisse sembler restrictive..."
├── ❌ "Bien que cette mesure peut sembler restrictive..." (indicatif = ERROR)
└── If unsure about subjunctive → Use "Même si" + INDICATIF instead

"Même si" + INDICATIVE
├── ✅ "Même si cette mesure peut sembler restrictive..."
├── ✅ "Même si certains s'y opposent..."
└── Safer grammatically than "bien que" — same logical function

"Malgré" + NOUN (no verb clause)
├── ✅ "Malgré les critiques, cette mesure reste nécessaire."
├── ❌ "Malgré que certains critiquent..." ("Malgré que" is debated/avoided in formal French)
└── "Malgré" is followed by a NOUN, not a clause

PATTERN 4: The Counter-Argument Paragraph (Advanced — Level 3 Preview)

Where: A dedicated paragraph in the body presenting the opposing view
Word cost: 40–70 words
Structure:

[Introduction]
[Argument 1 — YOUR position, fully developed]
[Counter-argument paragraph — OPPOSING view, briefly developed]
[Argument 2 / Rebuttal — YOUR response to the counter-argument]
[Conclusion]

Example — Ravi's body restructured with a counter-argument paragraph:

"Tout d'abord, cette interdiction contribuerait à la protection de l'environnement, car les sacs en plastique mettent plusieurs centaines d'années à se décomposer. Par conséquent, leur élimination réduirait considérablement la pollution des océans et des sols.

Certes, les opposants à cette mesure soulignent que l'interdiction pourrait engendrer des pertes d'emplois dans l'industrie plastique et que les alternatives, comme les sacs en coton, ont également un impact environnemental non négligeable.

Cependant, ces objections ne résistent pas à une analyse approfondie. En effet, la transition vers des matériaux durables créerait de nouveaux emplois dans les secteurs de l'innovation écologique. De surcroît, l'impact environnemental des alternatives réutilisables diminue significativement avec chaque utilisation répétée.

En définitive, malgré les réserves de certains, l'interdiction des sacs en plastique demeure une mesure écologiquement justifiée."

Why this works:

  • → Full paragraph dedicated to the opposing view → Shows deep bilateral thinking
  • → Followed by a REBUTTAL paragraph → Shows you can RESPOND to objections, not just ignore them
  • → "Certes... Cependant..." pattern structures the for-against-response flow
  • → This is a PREVIEW of Chapter 14's thèse-antithèse structure
  • → Total cost: ~60 extra words → Only feasible in responses of 220+ words

When to use Pattern 4:

  • When the prompt EXPLICITLY asks for both sides (Type 2: Advantages/Disadvantages)
  • When you are targeting a B2+ score and have sufficient word budget
  • When you are confident managing 3+ body paragraphs
  • NOT recommended for mini-responses or time-pressured situations

🔵 WHAT COULD BE DONE

Option A: Always use Pattern 1 (Conclusion Nuance)

  • → Safest and easiest approach
  • → Adds nuance with minimal word cost and structural disruption
  • → Sufficient for B1–B2 scoring
  • → Recommended as your DEFAULT strategy until you are comfortable with Patterns 2–4

Option B: Always use Pattern 4 (Counter-Argument Paragraph)

  • → Most impressive to the evaluator
  • → But highest risk — requires more words, more structural management, more time
  • → If poorly executed, it can CONFUSE your position rather than ENHANCE it
  • → Only use when you have practiced it thoroughly and have sufficient word budget

Option C: Choose the pattern based on the prompt type

  • → This is the OPTIMAL strategy for experienced candidates
  • → Matching pattern to prompt type maximizes effectiveness
PATTERN-PROMPT MATCHING:
│
├── Type 1 (Direct Opinion): Pattern 1 or 2 (brief acknowledgment is sufficient)
├── Type 2 (Advantages/Disadvantages): Pattern 4 (prompt DEMANDS both sides)
├── Type 3 (Cause/Solution): Pattern 1 (focus is on solutions, not debate)
├── Type 4 (Comparison): Pattern 2 or 3 (acknowledge the other option briefly)
├── Type 5 (Trend Evaluation): Pattern 2 (concede one downside of the trend)
├── Type 6 (Policy/Action): Pattern 2 or 4 (policy debates benefit from both sides)
└── Type 7 (Quote Reflection): Pattern 2 (acknowledge alternative interpretation)

Option D: Never acknowledge the other side — stay one-sided

  • → Technically not an error — the prompt asks for YOUR opinion
  • → But limits your Dimension 2 score ceiling
  • → Evaluators at B2 level expect nuance
  • → One-sided argumentation caps your score at low-to-mid B2
  • → Not recommended if your target is B2 or higher

✅ WHAT SHOULD DO

1. Always Run the Bilateral Thinking Protocol During Planning

ADDITION TO YOUR PLANNING NOTES (from Chapter 5):

PREVIOUS PLANNING FORMAT:
INTRO: [topic] → [problématique] → [thesis]
ARG 1: [reason] → [elaboration]
ARG 2: [reason] → [elaboration]
CONCL: [synthesis] → [ouverture]

NEW PLANNING FORMAT (with bilateral thinking):
INTRO: [topic] → [problématique] → [thesis]
ARG 1: [reason] → [elaboration]
COUNTER: [strongest opposing argument] → [my response to it]
ARG 2: [reason] → [elaboration]
CONCL: [synthesis] → [nuanced ouverture]

The COUNTER line takes 30 seconds to think of during planning
It transforms your response from one-sided to nuanced
This is the HIGHEST VALUE 30 seconds in your planning phase

2. Master the "Certes... Cependant..." Pair as Your Default Concession Tool

THE GOLDEN CONCESSION PAIR:
│
├── "Certes, [opposing point]."
│   ├── "Certes" = "Admittedly" / "Certainly" / "Granted"
│   ├── It signals: "I ACKNOWLEDGE this point has some validity"
│   ├── It does NOT mean: "I agree with this point"
│   └── It means: "I am aware of this objection and I take it seriously"
│
├── "Cependant, [your rebuttal or your stronger counter-point]."
│   ├── "Cependant" = "However"
│   ├── It signals: "DESPITE that acknowledgment, my position holds"
│   └── Everything AFTER "cependant" carries MORE weight than what came before "certes"
│
└── THE PSYCHOLOGICAL EFFECT ON THE EVALUATOR:
    ├── "Certes" shows INTELLECTUAL HONESTY → You are not ignoring objections
    ├── "Cependant" shows ARGUMENTATIVE STRENGTH → You can overcome objections
    └── Together they signal: MATURE, CONFIDENT, NUANCED argumentation

Practice drill:

CERTES-CEPENDANT DRILL:

For each topic below, write ONE "Certes... Cependant..." sentence pair:

1. Banning plastic bags
   "Certes, cette interdiction pourrait gêner certains consommateurs.
    Cependant, l'urgence écologique justifie cet effort d'adaptation."

2. Remote work
   "Certes, le télétravail peut engendrer un sentiment d'isolement.
    Cependant, il offre une flexibilité qui améliore la qualité de vie."

3. Mandatory sports in school
   "Certes, tous les élèves n'apprécient pas l'activité physique.
    Cependant, les bienfaits du sport sur la santé et la concentration sont indéniables."

4. Free public transport
   "Certes, la gratuité des transports représenterait un coût considérable pour les collectivités.
    Cependant, les retombées sociales et environnementales compenseraient largement cet investissement."

5. Social media for teenagers
   "Certes, les réseaux sociaux peuvent exposer les jeunes à des contenus inappropriés.
    Cependant, ils constituent également un outil d'apprentissage et de socialisation."

PRACTICE: Write 10 of these pairs on 10 different topics
→ This builds the concession reflex
→ After 10 pairs, the "Certes... Cependant..." pattern becomes automatic

3. Learn the Vocabulary of "Presenting the Other Side"

EXPRESSIONS FOR INTRODUCING THE OPPOSING VIEW:
│
├── REPORTING WHAT OTHERS THINK (distancing yourself from their view):
│   ├── "Certains estiment que..." (Some believe that...)
│   ├── "D'aucuns pourraient arguer que..." (Some might argue that...) [very formal]
│   ├── "Les opposants à cette mesure soulignent que..." (Opponents point out that...)
│   ├── "Il est parfois avancé que..." (It is sometimes argued that...)
│   └── "On pourrait objecter que..." (One could object that...)
│
├── ACKNOWLEDGING PARTIAL VALIDITY:
│   ├── "Certes, ..." (Admittedly...)
│   ├── "Il est vrai que..." (It is true that...)
│   ├── "Force est de reconnaître que..." (One must acknowledge that...)
│   ├── "On ne saurait nier que..." (One cannot deny that...)
│   └── "Il serait naïf d'ignorer que..." (It would be naive to ignore that...)
│
└── TRANSITIONING BACK TO YOUR POSITION:
    ├── "Cependant, ..." (However...)
    ├── "Néanmoins, ..." (Nevertheless...)
    ├── "Toutefois, ..." (However/Nevertheless...)
    ├── "Il n'en demeure pas moins que..." (It nonetheless remains that...)
    └── "Force est de constater que..." (One must observe that...) [from Chapter 3, Tier 3]

4. Follow the "Acknowledge → Concede → Overcome" Sequence

THE ACO SEQUENCE:
│
├── A — ACKNOWLEDGE: State the opposing argument clearly
│   "Certains estiment que l'interdiction des sacs en plastique
│    pénaliserait les petits commerçants."
│
├── C — CONCEDE: Admit the partial truth of this objection
│   "Il est vrai que cette transition impliquerait un coût initial
│    pour les commerces de proximité."
│
├── O — OVERCOME: Explain why your position is STILL stronger
│   "Cependant, les subventions gouvernementales et la demande croissante
│    pour des alternatives durables permettraient d'absorber ce coût
│    en quelques mois."
│
└── Total: 3 sentences → ~40–50 words → Fits within Pattern 2 or 3

NOTE: You do NOT always need all 3 steps
├── Minimum: A + O (Acknowledge + Overcome) → 2 sentences
├── Recommended: A + C + O (full sequence) → 3 sentences
└── Advanced: Embedded within 1 sentence using "Bien que" / "Même si" → Pattern 3

5. Ensure Your Position Remains DOMINANT

THE DOMINANCE RULE:
│
├── Your response should be approximately:
│   ├── 75–80% supporting YOUR position
│   └── 20–25% acknowledging the other side
│
├── In a 200-word response:
│   ├── ~150–160 words: Your arguments (intro + 2 supporting units + conclusion)
│   └── ~40–50 words: Opposing view acknowledgment + your response to it
│
├── If the opposing view takes MORE space than your supporting arguments:
│   → The evaluator may think you are arguing AGAINST your own thesis
│   → Dimension 1 risk: "Position unclear"
│
├── VISUAL TEST: If someone reads ONLY your first and last sentences,
│   can they identify your position?
│   ├── YES → Position is dominant ✅
│   └── NO → You have given too much space to the other side ❌
│
└── THE RULE: The other side is a GUEST in your response — it visits briefly
    Your position is the HOST — it occupies the house

❌ WHAT SHOULD NOT DO

1. Do NOT Present the Opposing Side as Equally Valid to Your Own

  • You are asked for YOUR opinion — not for a neutral analysis
  • ❌ "D'un côté, l'interdiction est bénéfique. D'un autre côté, elle est problématique. Les deux perspectives sont valables."
  • → This is FENCE-SITTING, not argumentation
  • → The evaluator reads: "This candidate cannot take a position"
  • → Dimension 1 penalized: The prompt asks you to CHOOSE
  • ✅ "Certes, certaines objections existent. Cependant, les bénéfices l'emportent clairement."
  • → You ACKNOWLEDGE the other side but CLEARLY favor your own

2. Do NOT Develop the Opposing Argument More Than Your Own

  • If you write 3 sentences developing the counterargument and only 2 sentences for your own argument
  • → The response feels like you are arguing AGAINST yourself
  • → Maximum development for the opposing side: 1–2 sentences (in Patterns 1–3)
  • → Your own arguments: 3–4 sentences each (fully developed justification units from Chapter 4)

3. Do NOT Introduce the Opposing View in the Introduction

  • The introduction announces YOUR position (Chapter 5, Slot C)
  • If you introduce the opposing view in the introduction:
    • → The reader is confused about which side you support
    • → Your thesis becomes diluted before you even start arguing
  • ❌ "Je pense que l'interdiction est bénéfique, mais certains pensent le contraire et ils ont peut-être raison."
  • → "Ils ont peut-être raison" UNDERMINES your thesis before the body even begins
  • ✅ Save the opposing view for the BODY or CONCLUSION

4. Do NOT Use "Mais" to Transition From Opposing View to Your Position

  • As established in Chapter 7: "Mais" is basic (A2 register)
  • After a concession, use formal opposition connectors:
    • → "Cependant, ..."
    • → "Néanmoins, ..."
    • → "Toutefois, ..."
    • → "Il n'en demeure pas moins que..."
  • ❌ "Certes, certains s'opposent. Mais je pense que c'est bien."
  • ✅ "Certes, certains s'opposent à cette mesure. Néanmoins, ses bénéfices environnementaux sont considérables."

5. Do NOT Argue Against Your OWN Position Convincingly

  • This sounds paradoxical, but it happens frequently
  • Candidates who are too good at presenting the counterargument accidentally CONVINCE the reader of the other side
  • The rule: Your opposing view presentation should be FACTUAL and BRIEF
  • Your OWN arguments should be DEVELOPED and PERSUASIVE
  • Persuasive language (strong vocabulary, detailed evidence) = reserved for YOUR side
  • Neutral, factual language = used for the OTHER side
LANGUAGE REGISTER FOR EACH SIDE:

YOUR POSITION (persuasive):
├── Strong verbs: "constitue," "représente," "s'avère," "garantit"
├── Positive/powerful adjectives: "indispensable," "considérable," "majeur," "fondamental"
├── Developed with evidence and examples
└── Conviction markers: "Je suis fermement convaincu," "Il est indéniable"

THE OTHER SIDE (neutral/reporting):
├── Distancing verbs: "certains estiment," "on pourrait objecter," "il est parfois avancé"
├── Neutral adjectives: "possible," "envisageable," "compréhensible"
├── Stated briefly without deep development
└── No conviction markers — you are REPORTING, not ENDORSING

6. Do NOT Forget to RETURN to Your Position After the Concession

  • The most dangerous moment is AFTER acknowledging the other side
  • If you concede and then move to the conclusion → The last argument the reader remembers is the OPPOSING one
  • ❌ Flow: Argument 1 → Concession → Conclusion (your last argument was the CONCESSION)
  • ✅ Flow: Argument 1 → Concession → YOUR REBUTTAL/Argument 2 → Conclusion (your last argument was YOURS)
  • The rule: Never let the opposing view be the LAST argument before the conclusion
  • Always follow a concession with your OWN stronger point

🚫 WHEN NOT TO USE

When NOT to Use Bilateral Thinking

  • When the prompt does NOT ask for your opinion but asks for information
    • → Example: "Décrivez les avantages du sport" → This asks you to DESCRIBE, not DEBATE
    • → No need for counterarguments — just present the advantages
    • → This is rare in Section B but possible

When NOT to Use Pattern 4 (Full Counter-Argument Paragraph)

  • In mini-responses → Not enough word budget
  • When you have only practiced Patterns 1–2 → Pattern 4 requires structural confidence
  • When the prompt is a simple direct opinion question → Full counter-argument paragraph feels disproportionate
  • When time pressure is high → Pattern 4 takes more planning and writing time

When NOT to Use "Bien que" (Embedded Concession)

  • When you are not confident with subjunctive conjugation
  • "Bien que" REQUIRES subjunctive → Grammar error here is very visible → Dimension 4 penalty
  • If unsure → Use "Même si" + indicative (same logical function, safer grammar)
  • Or use "Certes... Cependant..." (no subjunctive needed)

├── Many prompts implicitly expect nuance ("Justifiez" = justify, not just assert)
├── Bilateral thinking helps you FULLY address the prompt's expectations

DIMENSION 2 — COHÉRENCE/COHÉSION:
├── The "Certes... Cependant..." pattern creates a LOGICAL MOVEMENT
│   (concession → rebuttal → stronger position)
├── This movement shows SOPHISTICATED coherence
├── One-sided argumentation shows LINEAR coherence (simpler)
└── Bilateral coherence scores higher than linear coherence

DIMENSION 3 — LEXIQUE:
├── Acknowledging the other side introduces NEW VOCABULARY:
│   ├── Reporting expressions: "Certains estiment," "On pourrait objecter"

│   └── These are ALL high-register vocabulary items → Score boost
└── One-sided argumentation limits vocabulary range

DIMENSION 4 — GRAMMAIRE:
├── Concessive structures trigger advanced grammar:
│   ├── "Bien que" + subjunctive
│   ├── "Même si" + indicative
│   ├── Conditional: "On pourrait objecter que..."
│   └── These demonstrate grammatical range → Score boost
└── One-sided argumentation may not naturally trigger these structures

The Psychological Mechanism of Persuasion Through Concession

PERSUASION PSYCHOLOGY:
│
├── ONE-SIDED ARGUMENT:
│   ├── Reader thinks: "This person is only showing me one side"
│   ├── Reader's natural response: SKEPTICISM
│   ├── "What about the problems? Are they hiding something?"
│   └── Even if the argument is strong, doubt lingers
│
├── TWO-SIDED ARGUMENT WITH CLEAR POSITION:
│   ├── Reader thinks: "This person knows the objections and STILL maintains their position"
│   ├── Reader's natural response: TRUST
│   ├── "They have considered the counterarguments and their position survived"
│   └── The argument feels more CREDIBLE because it was tested against objections
│
└── THIS IS WHY CONCESSION STRENGTHENS YOUR ARGUMENT:
    ├── It is counterintuitive — admitting a weakness should weaken you
    ├── But psychologically, it INCREASES your credibility
    ├── Because it shows HONESTY and THOROUGHNESS
    └── The evaluator, as a reader, experiences this psychological effect

The Structural Mechanics of Where the Other Side Goes

FLOW DYNAMICS:
│
├── WRONG PLACEMENT — Other side at the END:
│   Introduction → Arg 1 (yours) → Arg 2 (yours) → Counter-argument → Conclusion
│   │
│   └── Problem: The LAST THING the reader processes before the conclusion
│       is the OPPOSING view → This view has RECENCY BIAS → It lingers
│       → Your conclusion has to fight against the reader's last impression
│
├── CORRECT PLACEMENT — Other side in the MIDDLE:
│   Introduction → Arg 1 (yours) → Counter-argument → Rebuttal (yours) → Conclusion
│   │
│   └── Advantage: Your arguments SURROUND the opposing view
│       → The reader starts with YOUR view (primacy effect)
│       → Encounters the objection
│       → IMMEDIATELY sees your response to it (recency effect)
│       → Your conclusion reinforces an already-won argument
│
└── ALSO CORRECT — Other side EMBEDDED within your argument:
    Introduction → Arg 1 (yours) → Arg 2 with embedded concession (yours) → Conclusion
    │
    └── Advantage: The opposing view never gets its own space
        → It exists only as a subordinate clause within YOUR argument
        → Maximum dominance of your position
        → Most elegant approach (Pattern 3)

🕳️ COMMON PITFALLS AND FAILURE MODES

Pitfall 1: The Accidental Debate

  • What happens: Candidate presents both sides so evenly that the response reads like a neutral report, not an opinion piece
  • Why it happens: Candidate misunderstands "nuance" as "neutrality"
  • How to prevent: Nuance ≠ Neutrality → Nuance = Clear position WITH awareness of complexity → Always ensure your position is identifiable and dominant (75–80% rule)

Pitfall 2: The Self-Defeating Concession

  • What happens: The concession is SO strong that it undermines the candidate's own thesis
  • Example: "Certes, l'interdiction des sacs en plastique détruirait des milliers d'emplois et causerait une crise économique majeure. Cependant, c'est bien pour l'environnement."
  • → The concession (detailed, dramatic: "milliers d'emplois," "crise économique majeure") is MORE convincing than the rebuttal (vague: "c'est bien")
  • How to prevent: Keep concessions BRIEF and FACTUAL → Keep rebuttals DEVELOPED and PERSUASIVE → The rebuttal must always be STRONGER than the concession

Pitfall 3: The Forgotten Return

  • What happens: Candidate concedes and then moves directly to the conclusion without returning to their own position
  • Example: "...Certes, cette mesure a des inconvénients. En définitive, cette question est complexe."
  • → Where is the REBUTTAL? Where is the candidate's OWN stronger argument?
  • → The last body content was the OPPOSING view → Conclusion feels weak
  • How to prevent: ALWAYS follow a concession with your own argument before concluding → Never let the opposing view be the last body content

Pitfall 4: The Contradictory Conclusion

  • What happens: Introduction says "I am FOR X" but conclusion says "Both sides have merit"
  • Why it happens: The bilateral thinking confused the candidate about their OWN position
  • How to prevent: Your conclusion must MATCH your introduction's position (Chapter 5 rule) → If your introduction says FOR → Your conclusion says FOR (with nuance, but still FOR)

Pitfall 5: The Forced Counterargument

  • What happens: Candidate cannot think of a genuine opposing argument, so they invent a weak, unrealistic one
  • Example: "Certains pourraient dire que la pollution n'est pas un problème."
  • → This is not a credible opposing argument — almost no one argues pollution is not a problem
  • → The evaluator sees: "This candidate is creating a straw man, not engaging with real objections"
  • How to prevent: Think of the STRONGEST objection, not the weakest → If you cannot think of ANY objection → Use Pattern 1 (Conclusion Nuance with a general statement) → Better than a fake counterargument

Pitfall 6: The "Bien que" Grammar Trap

  • What happens: Candidate uses "Bien que" with indicative instead of subjunctive
  • Example: ❌ "Bien que cette mesure est controversée..." → MUST be "soit" (subjunctive)
  • ✅ "Bien que cette mesure soit controversée..."
  • Why it matters: "Bien que" + wrong mood = highly visible grammar error → Dimension 4 penalty
  • How to prevent: If uncertain about subjunctive → Use "Même si" + indicative (identical logical function, no subjunctive risk) → Or use "Certes... Cependant..." (no mood issue at all)

Practice Scaling for Bilateral Thinking

STAGE 1: Bilateral Brainstorming (no writing)
├── Take 10 prompts from Chapter 6's practice bank
├── For each, list:
│   ├── 3 arguments FOR
│   └── 3 arguments AGAINST
├── Do NOT write a response — just brainstorm both sides
├── This trains the THINKING skill separately from the WRITING skill
└── Time: 3 minutes per prompt

STAGE 2: Certes-Cependant Drill (sentence-level)
├── For each of the 10 prompts above
├── Write ONE "Certes... Cependant..." sentence pair
├── Practice variety: Use different vocabulary each time
└── Time: 2 minutes per pair

STAGE 3: Pattern Integration (response-level)
├── Write 5 mini-responses using Pattern 1 (Conclusion Nuance)
├── Write 5 full responses using Pattern 2 (Concession Bridge)
├── Write 3 full responses using Pattern 3 (Embedded Concession)
├── Write 2 full responses using Pattern 4 (Counter-Argument Paragraph)
└── Progressive difficulty: Pattern 1 → 2 → 3 → 4

STAGE 4: Adaptive Selection (exam simulation)
├── Receive a prompt without knowing its type
├── Decode it (Chapter 2)
├── Decide which Pattern (1, 2, 3, or 4) fits best
├── Write the complete response with the chosen pattern
└── This trains STRATEGIC SELECTION — the exam-day skill

Bilateral Thinking Impact on Word Budget

WORD BUDGET IMPACT BY PATTERN:

PATTERN 1 (Conclusion Nuance):
├── Extra words needed: 10–20
├── Where they come from: Conclusion expands slightly
├── Body impact: NONE
└── Total response: ~210–250 words (minimal change)

PATTERN 2 (Concession Bridge):
├── Extra words needed: 20–40
├── Where they come from: Transition area between arguments
├── Body impact: Argument 2 may need slight compression
└── Total response: ~220–260 words (slight expansion)

PATTERN 3 (Embedded Concession):
├── Extra words needed: 10–15
├── Where they come from: Integrated into existing sentences
├── Body impact: MINIMAL — concession is woven in
└── Total response: ~210–250 words (negligible change)

PATTERN 4 (Counter-Argument Paragraph):
├── Extra words needed: 40–70
├── Where they come from: New paragraph added to body
├── Body impact: Either compress other arguments OR expand total length
└── Total response: ~250–300 words (significant expansion)
