🟡 HOW TO APPROACH IT

Step 1: Understand the Assembly Problem

  • Kenji's problem is NOT a knowledge problem — it is an ASSEMBLY problem
  • He knows how to build each component individually
  • But he does not know how to CONTROL SCOPE during live writing
  • This is like a chef who can make perfect sauce, perfect pasta, and perfect salad — but puts ALL the sauce on one plate and runs out for the other dishes
THE ASSEMBLY CHALLENGE:
│
├── Each component (from Chapters 2–5) was learned in ISOLATION
├── In isolation, there is no word count pressure
├── In assembly, every word spent on one component REDUCES words available for others
├── This is a ZERO-SUM GAME within 200–250 words
│
├── Kenji's breakdown:
│   ├── Introduction: 55 words (over-invested — should be ~35)
│   ├── Argument 1: 90 words (over-invested — should be ~60)
│   ├── Argument 2: 0 words (starved)
│   └── Conclusion: 2 words (starved)
│
└── The fix: BUDGET your words BEFORE writing

Step 2: Understand the "Mini-Response First" Strategy

  • Before writing a full 200–250 word response, you must master the MINI-RESPONSE
  • A mini-response is a COMPLETE response in only ~80–120 words
  • It contains ALL 3 parts (Introduction → Body → Conclusion)
  • It contains ONLY 1 justification unit (not 2)
  • It is deliberately SHORT — but STRUCTURALLY COMPLETE
WHY START WITH A MINI-RESPONSE:
│
├── It forces you to practice PROPORTION CONTROL in a smaller space
├── It proves that structure can exist even in limited word counts
├── It builds the ASSEMBLY HABIT before scaling up
├── It removes the intimidation of "I must write 250 words"
├── It shows you that a COMPLETE 80-word response scores better
│   than an INCOMPLETE 200-word response
│
└── Analogy: A pilot practices landing on a short runway before attempting a long one
    The skill is the same — the scale is different

Step 3: Define the Mini-Response Word Budget

MINI-RESPONSE WORD BUDGET (80–120 words total):
│
├── INTRODUCTION: 20–30 words (2 sentences maximum)
│   ├── Slot A + B combined into 1 sentence (context + problématique)
│   └── Slot C: Thesis (1 sentence — Chapter 3 opinion marker)
│
├── BODY: 40–60 words (3 sentences)
│   ├── 1 justification unit ONLY (from Chapter 4)
│   ├── Sentence 1: Category 1 connector + Specific reason
│   ├── Sentence 2: Category 3 expression + Elaboration
│   └── Sentence 3: Implication or brief example
│
└── CONCLUSION: 15–25 words (1–2 sentences)
    ├── Slot X: Synthesis (1 sentence — same position, different words)
    └── Slot Y: Ouverture → SKIP in mini-response (not enough room)

❌ WHAT SHOULD NOT DO

1. Do NOT Skip the Mini-Response Stage and Jump Directly to Full Responses

  • Full responses (200–250 words) involve managing MORE components simultaneously
  • If you cannot assemble 80 words with correct structure → 250 words will be worse, not better
  • Mini-responses build the assembly HABIT at manageable scale
  • Skipping this stage = building on an unstable foundation

2. Do NOT Spend More Than 3 Minutes Planning a Mini-Response

  • Mini-responses are SHORT → Planning should be proportionally short
  • 1 minute for prompt decoding
  • 1 minute for position + reason selection
  • 1 minute for connector choices
  • Total planning: 3 minutes maximum
  • Over-planning a mini-response defeats the purpose of building assembly SPEED

3. Do NOT Sacrifice Structure to Increase Word Count

  • ❌ Writing 150 words of body with no introduction and no conclusion
  • ✅ Writing 80 words with clear introduction, body, AND conclusion
  • The evaluator values STRUCTURE over VOLUME
  • A complete 80-word response > An incomplete 150-word response
  • This was Kenji's exact error — he chased word count and lost structure

4. Do NOT Reuse the Same Prompt More Than Once

  • Practicing the same prompt repeatedly teaches you to write THAT response
  • It does NOT teach you the ASSEMBLY PROCESS
  • Each practice should use a DIFFERENT prompt
  • The goal is process fluency, not product memorization

5. Do NOT Compare Your Mini-Response to a Full Model Response and Feel Discouraged

  • Mini-responses are DELIBERATELY shorter
  • They are a TRAINING tool, not the final performance
  • A mini-response with 1 argument is structurally identical to a full response with 2 arguments
  • The SKELETON is the same → The FLESH (additional argument) comes later

6. Do NOT Ignore the Self-Scoring Checklist

  • Writing without evaluating = practicing without feedback
  • Feedback is what creates IMPROVEMENT
  • Every mini-response you write should be followed by the self-scoring checklist
  • This builds SELF-AWARENESS — the ability to see your own strengths and weaknesses
  • Without self-awareness, you repeat the same errors indefinitely

⚙️ HOW IT ONLY WORKS (INTERNAL MECHANICS)

The Assembly Cognitive Load Problem

COGNITIVE LOAD THEORY APPLIED TO TEF WRITING:
│
├── Your brain has LIMITED working memory
├── During writing, your brain simultaneously manages:
│   ├── CONTENT: What am I saying?
│   ├── LANGUAGE: How do I say it in French?
│   ├── GRAMMAR: Is this conjugation correct?
│   ├── STRUCTURE: Where am I in the blueprint?
│   ├── PROPORTION: How many words have I used?
│   └── TIME: How many minutes do I have left?
│
├── That is 6 SIMULTANEOUS DEMANDS on working memory
├── Working memory can handle approximately 4 items at once
├── This means: OVERLOAD is guaranteed during first attempts
│
├── Kenji's overload pattern:
│   ├── He focused on CONTENT + LANGUAGE + GRAMMAR (3 items)
│   ├── He LOST TRACK of STRUCTURE + PROPORTION + TIME (3 items)
│   └── Result: Good sentences, terrible structure
│
└── THE SOLUTION: Reduce cognitive load through AUTOMATION
    ├── If STRUCTURE is automated (practiced until unconscious) → 1 less item to manage
    ├── If PROPORTION is automated (checkpoints become instinctive) → 1 less item
    ├── If CONNECTOR CHOICES are automated (arsenal memorized) → Less language load
    └── Mini-responses AUTOMATE structure and proportion at small scale
        → Then scaling to full responses adds only 1 more argument
        → NOT a complete restructuring of cognitive demands

🕳️ COMMON PITFALLS AND FAILURE MODES

Pitfall 1: The Word Count Obsession

  • What happens: Candidate counts words after every sentence, losing creative flow
  • Why it happens: Fear of exceeding or falling short of target
  • How to prevent: Use the CHECKPOINT system (check at 4 key moments, not after every sentence) → Between checkpoints, write freely

Pitfall 2: The Perfect First Sentence Paralysis

  • What happens: Candidate spends 5 minutes trying to write the perfect opening sentence
  • Why it happens: Knowledge of Tier 3 markers creates pressure to be impressive from word one
  • How to prevent:
    • → Write ANY grammatically correct opening sentence first
    • → You can improve it during the review phase
    • → A good sentence written in 30 seconds > A perfect sentence that takes 5 minutes
    • → Progress > Perfection during the writing phase

Pitfall 3: The "I Know This Topic" Trap

  • What happens: Candidate knows a lot about the topic and wants to include everything
  • Why it happens: Enthusiasm + knowledge create the urge to show expertise
  • How to prevent:
    • → Remind yourself: TEF tests WRITING SKILL, not TOPIC KNOWLEDGE
    • → Choose your BEST 1 reason (for mini) or 2 reasons (for full) and DEVELOP them deeply
    • → Reject all other reasons, no matter how good they are
    • → Depth on 1–2 reasons > Breadth on 5 reasons (reinforcing Chapter 4's principle)

Pitfall 4: The Connector Overload

  • What happens: Candidate uses 6–7 different connectors in 80 words
  • Why it happens: After learning Chapter 4's arsenal, the candidate wants to demonstrate everything
  • How to prevent:
    • → Mini-response: 3–4 connectors maximum
    • → Full response: 6–8 connectors maximum
    • → Connectors should feel NATURAL, not forced
    • → If every sentence starts with a connector → It feels mechanical → Reduce

Pitfall 5: The Assembly Order Confusion

  • What happens: Candidate writes the conclusion first, then the body, then the introduction
  • Why it happens: Candidate is unsure where to start and writes whatever comes to mind first
  • How to prevent:
    • → For beginners: ALWAYS write in order (Introduction → Body → Conclusion)
    • → Sequential writing builds structural awareness
    • → Out-of-order writing is only viable for advanced writers (Chapter 26 discusses this)
    • → At this stage: Order = Safety

Pitfall 6: The "It Doesn't Sound French" Doubt

  • What happens: Candidate writes a structurally correct response but doubts it sounds natural
  • Why it happens: The formal structures from Chapters 3–5 feel "textbook-like" to the candidate
  • How to prevent:
    • → Formal written French IS supposed to sound "textbook-like"
    • → This is NOT spoken French — it is argumentative essay writing
    • → "Il me semble que" + "En effet" + "Cela signifie que" = EXACTLY what the evaluator expects
    • → What feels unnatural to you feels PROFESSIONAL to the evaluator
    • → Trust the system

What Chapter 6 Established

ASSEMBLY is a separate skill from COMPONENT BUILDING:
├── Knowing how to form opinions (Ch.3) ≠ Knowing how to integrate them into a response
├── Knowing how to justify (Ch.4) ≠ Knowing how to control proportion while justifying
├── Knowing the structure (Ch.5) ≠ Knowing how to fill the structure under time pressure
└── Assembly requires PRACTICE — specifically, repeated practice of the FULL PROCESS

The Mini-Response (80–120 words) is the training format:
├── Contains ALL 3 structural parts (Introduction → Body → Conclusion)
├── Contains 1 justification unit (not 2)
├── Teaches proportion control at manageable scale
├── Builds assembly fluency before scaling to full length
└── Serves as an emergency fallback strategy during the actual exam

The Assembly Process (5 steps):
├── Step 1: Decode prompt (Chapter 2) — 2 minutes
├── Step 2: Choose position + opinion marker (Chapter 3) — 1 minute
├── Step 3: Select reason + connectors (Chapter 4) — 2 minutes
├── Step 4: Write Introduction → Body → Conclusion (Chapter 5) — 15–25 minutes
└── Step 5: Review with self-scoring checklist — 3–5 minutes

Word Count Checkpoint System prevents proportion collapse:
├── After introduction: ~30–50 words
├── After body unit 1: ~90–120 words
├── After body unit 2: ~170–210 words (full responses)
└── After conclusion: ~200–250 words (full responses)

KEY PRINCIPLE: Completeness > Length
├── A complete 100-word response outscores an incomplete 180-word response
└── Structure is a MULTIPLIER (Chapter 5) — zero structure = zero multiplication
