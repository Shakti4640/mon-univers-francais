🟡 HOW TO APPROACH IT

Step 1: Understand Why Self-Assessment Is a Separate Skill

  • Writing ability and evaluation ability are TWO DIFFERENT cognitive skills
  • You can be a strong writer who is a poor self-evaluator
  • You can be a weak writer who accurately identifies weaknesses
  • TEF preparation requires BOTH skills working together
  • Most candidates develop writing skills but never develop evaluation skills
  • Result: they cannot self-correct, they repeat the same mistakes, they plateau

Step 2: Understand Why Generic Self-Assessment Fails

Generic AssessmentWhy It Fails
"My response was good"Good by WHAT criteria? Which dimensions? Compared to what standard?
"I need to improve vocabulary"WHICH vocabulary? Topic-specific? Connectors? Formal register? Precision?
"My grammar has some errors"Which errors? Systematic patterns or random mistakes? Accord? Tense? Structure?
"I think I would score around 280"Based on what? Wishful thinking or dimensional analysis?
  • Generic assessment produces generic improvement plans
  • Generic improvement plans produce generic practice
  • Generic practice produces marginal gains
  • Precision in diagnosis → Precision in practice → Precision in score improvement

Step 3: Understand the Full Simulation Architecture

  • A complete battle simulation has THREE phases, not just one:
PHASE A: EXECUTION (35 minutes)
→ Simulate real exam conditions with absolute fidelity
→ Produce a complete response under authentic time pressure

PHASE B: DIMENSIONAL SCORING (20 minutes)
→ Evaluate your response across all 4 TEF dimensions
→ Assign specific level scores to each dimension
→ Calculate a composite assessment

PHASE C: GAP ANALYSIS + PRESCRIPTION (15 minutes)
→ Identify the SPECIFIC weaknesses limiting your score
→ Create a targeted practice plan for the next session
→ Track progress across multiple simulations

PHASE A: EXECUTION — THE FULL BATTLE SIMULATION

🔹 A1: SETTING UP AUTHENTIC CONDITIONS

Environmental Fidelity

REQUIRED CONDITIONS:
  ✓ Quiet room — no interruptions
  ✓ Phone OFF (not silent — OFF or in another room)
  ✓ No dictionary, no translator, no grammar reference
  ✓ No internet access
  ✓ Paper and pen ONLY (or computer with all other applications closed)
  ✓ A visible clock or timer
  ✓ No background music
  ✓ No food or drink preparation during the 35 minutes

PROHIBITED:
  ✗ Pausing the timer for any reason
  ✗ Looking up a word "just this once"
  ✗ Starting over with a new prompt if the first one is difficult
  ✗ Extending time by "just 2 more minutes"
  ✗ Having notes, vocabulary lists, or chapter summaries visible

Why Environmental Fidelity Is Non-Negotiable

  • Every accommodation you allow yourself in practice creates a false competence signal
  • If you practice with a dictionary and score well → you believe your vocabulary is adequate → on exam day without a dictionary, vocabulary collapses
  • If you practice without time pressure → you believe your planning is efficient → on exam day, time runs out
  • The simulation must hurt slightly to be useful
  • Discomfort during practice = comfort during the real exam

Minute-by-Minute Battle Plan

             → First read: general understanding

             → Individual lens: Workers displaced vs. workers upskilled

             → Pull from Technology theme (Chapter 18): l'automatisation, le numérique, les algorithmes

              → Write abbreviated plan on scratch paper

MINUTES 10–30: WRITING (20 minutes of continuous production)
               → Introduction: 2-3 sentences (40-50 words)
               → Body thèse: 3-4 sentences (60-70 words)
               → Body antithèse: 3-4 sentences (60-70 words)

Model Response — C1 Level (Target: 316+)

L'essor fulgurant de l'intelligence artificielle suscite des interrogations légitimes quant à l'avenir du monde professionnel. Si certains y voient une menace existentielle pour l'emploi, d'autres y perçoivent le ferment d'une transformation bénéfique. Il me semble que la réalité se situe dans un entre-deux qui exige une analyse nuancée.

Force est de reconnaître que l'automatisation engendrée par l'IA met en péril un nombre considérable de métiers fondés sur des tâches répétitives. Le secteur manufacturier, les services administratifs et même certaines professions intellectuelles — telles que la traduction ou l'analyse de données — subissent déjà les effets de cette mutation. Il ne s'agit pas d'un scénario hypothétique mais d'une réalité économique observable.

Toutefois, réduire l'IA à un simple destructeur d'emplois relèverait d'une lecture partielle de l'histoire technologique. Chaque révolution industrielle a certes supprimé des métiers, mais en a simultanément engendré de nouveaux, souvent imprévisibles. L'IA crée d'ores et déjà des professions inédites — ingénieurs en apprentissage automatique, spécialistes en éthique algorithmique — qui n'existaient pas il y a une décennie.

En définitive, l'IA ne constitue ni une fatalité ni une panacée : c'est notre capacité collective à anticiper et à encadrer cette transformation qui déterminera si elle représente une menace ou une chance.

PHASE B: DIMENSIONAL SCORING — THE SELF-EVALUATION RUBRIC

🔹 B1: THE COMPLETE SELF-SCORING RUBRIC

How to Use This Rubric

  • Wait at least 30 minutes after writing before scoring
  • This temporal distance prevents the "writer's bias" — you are too close to your own text immediately after writing
  • Read your response as if someone ELSE wrote it
  • Score each dimension INDEPENDENTLY — do not let one dimension influence another
  • Be HONEST — generous self-scoring produces false confidence

DIMENSION 1: RESPECT DE LA CONSIGNE

LevelCriteriaSelf-Check Questions
Level 0Does not address the prompt at all OR writes a completely different type of textDid I write about the topic in the prompt? Did I write an argumentative text?
Level 1Partially addresses the prompt — misses key elements (no examples, no clear position, wrong text type)Did I state a clear position? Did I include at least one example? Did I address ALL parts of the instructions?
Level 2Addresses the prompt adequately — position stated, some arguments and examples present, approximately correct lengthIs my position identifiable? Are my arguments directly relevant to the question? Am I within 170–270 words?
Level 3Fully addresses the prompt — clear position, well-developed arguments, relevant examples, appropriate lengthDoes every paragraph contribute to answering the specific question? Are my examples concrete and specific, not vague?
Level 4Addresses the prompt with sophistication — engages deeply with the question, redefines terms if needed, examples are precise and variedDid I engage with the COMPLEXITY of the question, not just its surface? Did I demonstrate understanding of WHY this question is debatable?

SCORING EXERCISE FOR DIMENSION 1

Read your response and answer EACH question below with YES or NO:

□ I wrote about the topic stated in the prompt
□ I wrote an argumentative text (not a letter, story, or description)
□ I stated a clear position (even if nuanced)
□ I provided at least 2 distinct arguments
□ I included at least 1 concrete example
□ I addressed ALL instruction elements (opinion + justification + examples)
□ My word count is between 180 and 270
□ Every paragraph relates directly to the prompt question
□ I did not drift to a different topic mid-response

SCORING:
  0-3 YES answers → Level 1
  4-5 YES answers → Level 2
  6-7 YES answers → Level 3
  8-9 YES answers → Level 4

LevelCriteriaSelf-Check Questions

SCORING EXERCISE FOR DIMENSION 2

Read your response and perform EACH check:

STRUCTURAL CHECKS:
□ Introduction is present and identifiable (2-3 sentences)
□ Body paragraphs are present and separated
□ Conclusion is present and identifiable (2+ sentences)
□ Each body paragraph has ONE main function (thèse OR antithèse OR synthèse)

CONNECTOR CHECKS:
□ At least 4 different connectors used
□ No connector is used more than twice
□ Every connector accurately reflects the logical relationship it claims

FLOW CHECKS:
□ Sentence 2 logically follows Sentence 1 in every paragraph
□ No sentence feels randomly placed or disconnected
□ The conclusion does not introduce a completely new argument
□ No two paragraphs say the same thing in different words

SCORING:
  0-3 checks passed → Level 1
  4-6 checks passed → Level 2
  7-9 checks passed → Level 3
  10-12 checks passed → Level 4

🔵 WHAT COULD BE DONE

Option A: Run one simulation and consider yourself calibrated

  • → Minimum 5 simulations needed for reliable self-calibration
  • → One simulation reveals your CURRENT state — multiple simulations reveal your TRAJECTORY

Option B: Score yourself immediately after writing

  • → Writer's bias distorts scoring — you remember what you MEANT to write, not what you ACTUALLY wrote
  • → Wait 30 minutes minimum, ideally 24 hours
  • → Read with fresh eyes as if evaluating a stranger's work

Option C: Run simulations + self-score + track + prescribe + iterate

  • → This is the correct approach
  • → Each simulation cycle: Execute → Score → Diagnose → Prescribe → Practice → Execute again
  • → This creates a FEEDBACK LOOP that drives targeted improvement
  • → 5 simulation cycles in 2 weeks can produce more improvement than 2 months of unstructured practice

Option D: Skip self-scoring and rely entirely on tutor feedback

  • → Tutor feedback is valuable but often delayed and generic
  • → Self-scoring is immediate and can be dimensionally precise
  • → Best approach: self-score FIRST, then compare with tutor evaluation to calibrate your accuracy

1. Schedule Simulation Sessions Like Real Exams

  • Treat the session as non-negotiable
  • Create exam-like conditions every time
  • Minimum: 2 simulations per week in your final preparation phase

2. Use Different Prompts for Every Simulation

  • Never repeat a prompt
  • Vary across themes: technology, education, environment, work, health, culture, society

❌ WHAT SHOULD NOT DO

1. Do NOT Score Yourself Generously to Feel Better

  • Generous self-scoring feels good temporarily
  • It creates false confidence that collapses on exam day
  • If you are unsure whether your response is Level 2 or Level 3 → Score it Level 2
  • Underestimating your score is safer than overestimating it
  • If you score yourself at Level 2 and the real exam gives you Level 3 → pleasant surprise
  • If you score yourself at Level 3 and the real exam gives you Level 2 → devastating gap

2. Do NOT Skip Simulations Because "I Already Know My Weaknesses"

  • You THINK you know your weaknesses
  • But cognitive bias means you are often wrong about your own blind spots
  • The simulation REVEALS weaknesses you did not know you had
  • Every candidate who says "I already know what to fix" and skips simulation → surprised by exam results

3. Do NOT Change Your Strategy Between Simulations Without Targeted Practice

  • Simulation 1 reveals: weak Dimension 3 (vocabulary)
  • You immediately try Simulation 2 without practicing vocabulary
  • Simulation 2: still weak Dimension 3
  • You feel discouraged
  • The cycle must be: Simulate → Diagnose → PRACTICE THE SPECIFIC WEAKNESS → Simulate again
  • Without the practice step between simulations, you are just measuring the same weakness repeatedly

4. Do NOT Compare Your Responses to the C1 Model and Feel Defeated

  • The C1 model response in this chapter represents EXCELLENT performance
  • Very few candidates produce C1-level output on their first simulation
  • Your TRAJECTORY matters more than your starting point
  • Going from Level 2 → Level 3 across 5 simulations is EXCELLENT progress
  • Going from Level 2 → Level 2 across 5 simulations despite practice → indicates wrong practice approach, not insufficient talent

5. Do NOT Simulate More Than Once Per Day

  • Mental fatigue degrades performance on the second simulation
  • You cannot distinguish "genuine weakness" from "tiredness" if you double-simulate
  • One simulation per day, properly executed and evaluated, is worth more than three rushed ones

6. Do NOT Ignore the Plan Phase During Simulation

  • Some candidates skip planning during simulation to "save time" and "see how they do under real pressure"
  • This tests your panic response, not your writing ability

Why Dimensional Scoring Works Better Than Holistic Scoring

HOLISTIC SCORING:
  → "My response was about a 7/10"
  → What does this mean? Which parts are the 7? Which parts drag it down?
  → You cannot target improvement because you cannot locate weakness
  → Result: General practice → General improvement → Slow progress

DIMENSIONAL SCORING:
  → "Dimension 1: Level 3, Dimension 2: Level 3, Dimension 3: Level 2, Dimension 4: Level 3"
  → Clearly: vocabulary is the limiting factor
  → You can now spend 80% of practice time on vocabulary improvement
  → Result: Targeted practice → Specific improvement → Fast progress

THE MATH OF TARGETED IMPROVEMENT:
  → Improving your WEAKEST dimension from Level 2 to Level 3 
    raises your average by 0.25
  → Improving your STRONGEST dimension from Level 3 to Level 4 
    ALSO raises your average by 0.25
  → BUT improving a weak dimension is usually EASIER than 
    pushing a strong dimension higher
  → Therefore: ALWAYS prioritize your weakest dimension
  → This is the fastest path to score improvement

🕳️ COMMON PITFALLS AND FAILURE MODES

Pitfall 1: The Simulation Avoidance Pattern

  • Symptom: Candidate keeps studying grammar rules, vocabulary lists, model responses — but never actually WRITES a timed response
  • Cause: Fear of confronting actual performance level
  • Why it fails: Knowledge without timed execution = theoretical competence that collapses under exam pressure
  • Fix: Schedule your first simulation NOW — before you feel "ready" — you will never feel ready — the simulation itself creates readiness

Pitfall 2: The Scoring Inflation Pattern

  • Symptom: Candidate consistently self-scores 0.5–1.0 levels higher than professional evaluation
  • Cause: Emotional investment in own writing → difficulty seeing weaknesses
  • Why it fails: Creates false confidence → no targeted improvement → exam score disappoints
  • Fix: For your first 3 simulations, also have a tutor or qualified French speaker score the same response → Compare their scores to yours → Calibrate your bias

Pitfall 3: The Diagnosis Without Action Pattern

  • Symptom: Candidate runs simulations, scores accurately, identifies weaknesses — but never does targeted practice between simulations
  • Cause: The simulation itself feels productive → creates illusion of progress
  • Why it fails: Measuring a weakness 5 times does not fix it — only targeted practice fixes it
  • Fix: Between every two simulations, spend at least 2 hours on your weakest dimension's specific sub-weakness

Pitfall 4: The Perfectionism Paralysis Pattern

  • Symptom: Candidate scores Level 2 on Dimension 3, feels devastated, loses motivation to continue
  • Cause: Expecting immediate C1 performance → unrealistic self-expectations
  • Why it fails: Demoralization stops practice → practice stops improvement → self-fulfilling failure
  • Fix: Reframe: Level 2 is your STARTING POINT, not your ceiling — every simulation that identifies a Level 2 is a simulation that tells you EXACTLY what to improve — this is valuable information, not failure

Pitfall 5: The Over-Simulation Pattern

  • Symptom: Candidate runs simulations daily, sometimes twice daily, in the final week
  • Cause: Anxiety-driven hyperactivity → feeling that MORE simulations = MORE preparation
  • Why it fails: Mental fatigue degrades both writing quality and scoring accuracy → Results become unreliable → Candidate enters exam exhausted
  • Fix: Maximum 3 simulations per week → No simulation in the final 48 hours → Rest is preparation

Pitfall 6: The Model Response Memorization Trap

  • Symptom: After seeing the C1 model response in this chapter, candidate attempts to memorize it for use on exam day
  • Cause: Believing that reproducing excellent writing = being an excellent writer
  • Why it fails: The exam prompt will be DIFFERENT → Memorized content will not fit → Dimension 1 failure → Evaluators recognize memorized passages
  • Fix: Use the model ONLY for calibration ("What does Level 4 look like?") → Never for reproduction → Your own Level 3 writing that answers the actual prompt scores HIGHER than a memorized Level 4 passage that does not
