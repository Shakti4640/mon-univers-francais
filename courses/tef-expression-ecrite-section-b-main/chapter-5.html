📘 CHAPTER 5

THE SKELETON KEY: LEARNING THE UNIVERSAL 3-PART RESPONSE STRUCTURE

🔴 HYPOTHETICAL PROBLEM

You are a TEF candidate named Sofia
She studied Chapter 1 → She understands the 4 scoring dimensions
She studied Chapter 2 → She can decode any prompt using the 3-layer extraction protocol
She studied Chapter 3 → She can form opinion sentences at Tier 2–3 level
She studied Chapter 4 → She can build 3-sentence justification units with varied causal connectors
She sits down with this practice prompt:

"Avec le développement des nouvelles technologies, certains métiers traditionnels disparaissent. Pensez-vous que cette évolution soit préoccupante? Justifiez votre réponse à l'aide d'arguments et d'exemples."

Sofia applies her skills perfectly:

  • She decodes the prompt (Chapter 2): Topic = disappearing traditional jobs due to technology; Question = Is this worrying?; Type 1 + Type 5 (Opinion + Trend Evaluation)
  • She writes an excellent opinion sentence (Chapter 3): "Je suis fermement convaincue que la disparition de certains métiers traditionnels, bien que déstabilisante, constitue une évolution naturelle et globalement positive."
  • She writes two strong justification units (Chapter 4): One about new jobs replacing old ones, one about technology improving efficiency

But here is what her response looks like:

"Je suis fermement convaincue que la disparition de certains métiers traditionnels constitue une évolution naturelle. En effet, l'histoire montre que chaque révolution technologique a créé plus d'emplois qu'elle n'en a supprimés. Cela signifie que les travailleurs s'adaptent. Étant donné que la technologie améliore la productivité, les entreprises deviennent plus compétitives. En effet, l'automatisation permet de réduire les coûts. Cela signifie que l'économie bénéficie globalement de ces changements."

She reads it back. Something feels wrong.
There is no opening. No closing. No separation between arguments.
It reads like one continuous block of text — no breathing room.
The evaluator notes: "Absence d'introduction et de conclusion — structure non respectée — cohérence insuffisante"
Sofia scores poorly on Dimension 2 (Coherence/Cohesion) despite strong content

❓ THE CORE QUESTION

  • Sofia has all the right INGREDIENTS — but no RECIPE
  • She has opinion sentences and justification units but no organizational framework
  • What is the universal structure that every Section B response must follow?
  • How do you arrange content so the evaluator perceives logical organization?
  • What goes WHERE, and WHY does position matter?

🟡 HOW TO APPROACH IT

Step 1: Understand Why Structure Exists in French Argumentative Writing

  • French academic and argumentative writing follows a deeply rooted cultural tradition
  • This tradition values clarity, order, and logical progression above all
  • Unlike some writing cultures that value creative flow or emotional narrative
  • French writing culture says: "If the reader cannot see your plan, you have no plan"
  • TEF evaluators are trained within this tradition
  • They expect to SEE a visible structure — not guess at one
THE FRENCH WRITING PRINCIPLE:
├── ANNOUNCE what you will say (Introduction)
├── SAY it with organized support (Développement / Body)
├── CONFIRM what you said (Conclusion)
│
├── This is NOT optional decoration
├── This IS the fundamental architecture of credible French argumentation
└── Violating this = violating the evaluator's basic expectations

Step 2: Understand the Universal 3-Part Structure

PART 1: INTRODUCTION (L'introduction)
├── Function: Frame the debate and announce your position
├── Position in text: FIRST
├── Approximate length: 2–4 sentences (30–50 words)
└── Contains: Context + Thesis announcement

PART 2: BODY (Le développement)
├── Function: Present and develop your arguments
├── Position in text: MIDDLE
├── Approximate length: 6–10 sentences (120–160 words)
└── Contains: Justification units from Chapter 4

PART 3: CONCLUSION (La conclusion)
├── Function: Synthesize and close the argument
├── Position in text: LAST
├── Approximate length: 2–3 sentences (30–50 words)
└── Contains: Final position + synthesis (NOT repetition)

Step 3: Understand the PROPORTION Rule

  • Not all three parts are equal in length
  • The body is the DOMINANT section — it carries the arguments
  • Introduction and conclusion are SHORT but ESSENTIAL framing devices
IDEAL PROPORTION FOR 200–250 WORD RESPONSE:

Introduction: ~15–20% of total words → 30–50 words
Body:         ~60–70% of total words → 120–170 words
Conclusion:   ~15–20% of total words → 30–50 words

VISUAL PROPORTION:
████░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░████
INTRO        BODY (largest)        CONCL

Sofia's error: 100% body, 0% intro, 0% conclusion
Correct model: ~20% intro + ~65% body + ~15% conclusion

🟢 HOW CAN WE SOLVE IT

The Solution = A Fixed Architectural Blueprint With Slots to Fill

  • Think of the 3-part structure as a BUILDING with rooms
  • Each room has a SPECIFIC PURPOSE
  • You do not put the bathroom in the living room
  • Similarly, you do not put your conclusion content in the introduction
  • Each room has SLOTS — specific components that belong inside

🔹 PART 1: THE INTRODUCTION — Internal Architecture

INTRODUCTION BLUEPRINT:
│
├── SLOT A: L'AMORCE (The Hook / Context Opener)
│   ├── 1 sentence that introduces the GENERAL TOPIC
│   ├── Sets the scene for the reader
│   ├── Does NOT state your opinion yet
│   ├── Uses general, factual language
│   └── Purpose: Tells the evaluator "I understand what this debate is about"
│
├── SLOT B: LA PROBLÉMATIQUE (The Issue / Debate Framing)
│   ├── 1 sentence that narrows from general topic to SPECIFIC QUESTION
│   ├── Often echoes (but does NOT copy) the prompt's question
│   ├── Frames the tension or debate
│   └── Purpose: Tells the evaluator "I have identified the exact issue"
│
└── SLOT C: L'ANNONCE DU PLAN / THÈSE (Thesis Announcement)
    ├── 1 sentence stating YOUR POSITION (opinion sentence from Chapter 3)
    ├── This is where your Tier 2–3 opinion marker appears
    ├── Optionally previews HOW you will argue (not always necessary at TEF level)
    └── Purpose: Tells the evaluator "Here is where I stand"

Example Introduction Using This Blueprint:

SLOT A (Amorce): De nos jours, le développement rapide des nouvelles technologies transforme profondément le marché du travail.

SLOT B (Problématique): Face à la disparition de nombreux métiers traditionnels, il est légitime de se demander si cette évolution est véritablement préoccupante.

SLOT C (Thèse): Je suis fermement convaincue que, malgré les inquiétudes qu'elle suscite, cette transformation représente une évolution globalement positive pour la société.

Word count of this introduction: ~55 words → Perfect proportion

🔹 PART 2: THE BODY — Internal Architecture

BODY BLUEPRINT:
│
├── JUSTIFICATION UNIT 1 (Argument principal)
│   ├── From Chapter 4: Category 1 connector + Reason
│   ├── From Chapter 4: Category 3 expression + Elaboration
│   ├── From Chapter 4: Category 3 expression + Implication
│   ├── Optional: Concrete example (will be developed fully in Chapter 9)
│   ├── Approximate length: 50–80 words
│   └── Purpose: Your STRONGEST argument supporting your position
│
├── TRANSITION (optional but valuable)
│   ├── 1 short sentence or phrase linking Unit 1 to Unit 2
│   ├── Shows the evaluator that your arguments are CONNECTED, not random
│   ├── Examples: "Au-delà de cet aspect..." / "Par ailleurs..." / "De surcroît..."
│   └── Purpose: Maintains Dimension 2 (cohesion) between argument blocks
│
└── JUSTIFICATION UNIT 2 (Argument secondaire)
    ├── Same structure as Unit 1 but with DIFFERENT connectors (variety from Chapter 4)
    ├── Addresses a DIFFERENT angle of the question
    ├── Must NOT repeat the same point as Unit 1
    ├── Approximate length: 50–80 words
    └── Purpose: Your SECOND argument — broadens the support for your position

Example Body Using This Blueprint:

UNIT 1: Tout d'abord, l'histoire démontre que chaque révolution technologique a engendré davantage d'emplois qu'elle n'en a éliminés, car de nouveaux secteurs émergent pour remplacer ceux qui disparaissent. En effet, l'apparition de l'informatique a fait disparaître le métier de dactylographe, mais a simultanément créé des millions d'emplois dans le domaine du numérique. Cela signifie que le marché du travail possède une capacité d'adaptation remarquable.

TRANSITION: Par ailleurs, au-delà de cette dynamique de renouvellement professionnel, il convient de considérer les bénéfices en termes de qualité de vie.

UNIT 2: Étant donné que l'automatisation permet d'éliminer les tâches répétitives et physiquement éprouvantes, les travailleurs peuvent se concentrer sur des activités plus créatives et intellectuellement stimulantes. Autrement dit, la technologie libère le potentiel humain plutôt qu'elle ne le remplace. Cela s'explique par le fait que les compétences les plus difficiles à automatiser — la créativité, l'empathie, le jugement critique — sont précisément celles qui enrichissent l'expérience professionnelle.

Word count of this body: ~155 words → Perfect proportion

🔹 PART 3: THE CONCLUSION — Internal Architecture

CONCLUSION BLUEPRINT:
│
├── SLOT X: LA SYNTHÈSE (The Synthesis)
│   ├── 1–2 sentences that SUMMARIZE the essence of your argument
│   ├── NOT a copy-paste of your introduction or body
│   ├── A DISTILLATION — capture the core logic in fresh words
│   ├── Reaffirm your position WITHOUT using the same opinion marker as the introduction
│   └── Purpose: Confirms to the evaluator where you stand after developing your arguments
│
└── SLOT Y: L'OUVERTURE (The Opening / Broadening) — OPTIONAL
    ├── 1 sentence that broadens the discussion or suggests a related question
    ├── Shows intellectual curiosity and depth
    ├── Does NOT introduce a NEW argument (the argument phase is over)
    ├── Examples: A related question / A future perspective / A broader implication
    └── Purpose: Leaves the evaluator with the impression of a thoughtful, mature writer

Example Conclusion Using This Blueprint:

SLOT X (Synthèse): En définitive, la disparition de certains métiers traditionnels, loin d'être préoccupante, témoigne de la capacité de nos sociétés à évoluer et à s'adapter aux mutations technologiques.

SLOT Y (Ouverture): Toutefois, il serait essentiel de veiller à ce que cette transition s'accompagne de politiques de formation adéquates pour que chaque travailleur puisse bénéficier de ces transformations.

Word count of this conclusion: ~50 words → Perfect proportion

COMPLETE ASSEMBLED RESPONSE

De nos jours, le développement rapide des nouvelles technologies transforme profondément le marché du travail. Face à la disparition de nombreux métiers traditionnels, il est légitime de se demander si cette évolution est véritablement préoccupante. Je suis fermement convaincue que, malgré les inquiétudes qu'elle suscite, cette transformation représente une évolution globalement positive pour la société.

Tout d'abord, l'histoire démontre que chaque révolution technologique a engendré davantage d'emplois qu'elle n'en a éliminés, car de nouveaux secteurs émergent pour remplacer ceux qui disparaissent. En effet, l'apparition de l'informatique a fait disparaître le métier de dactylographe, mais a simultanément créé des millions d'emplois dans le domaine du numérique. Cela signifie que le marché du travail possède une capacité d'adaptation remarquable.

Par ailleurs, étant donné que l'automatisation permet d'éliminer les tâches répétitives et physiquement éprouvantes, les travailleurs peuvent se concentrer sur des activités plus créatives et intellectuellement stimulantes. Autrement dit, la technologie libère le potentiel humain plutôt qu'elle ne le remplace.

En définitive, la disparition de certains métiers traditionnels témoigne de la capacité de nos sociétés à évoluer. Toutefois, il serait essentiel de veiller à ce que cette transition s'accompagne de politiques de formation adéquates.

Total word count: ~210 words → Within target range ✅

🔵 WHAT COULD BE DONE

Option A: Write the body first, then add introduction and conclusion

  • → Actually a viable strategy during the WRITING phase
  • → Many skilled writers draft the body first because it is the core content
  • → Then craft the introduction to match what they actually wrote
  • → Then write a conclusion that synthesizes the body
  • → Risk: If you run out of time, you might have body but no intro/conclusion → Still penalized
  • → Mitigation: At minimum, write a 1-sentence intro and 1-sentence conclusion before time runs out

Option B: Write introduction → body → conclusion in order

  • → The most natural and safest approach for most candidates
  • → Ensures you START with the framework and fill it in
  • → Lower risk of missing a section
  • → Recommended for candidates at B1–B2 level

Option C: Skip the introduction entirely and start with arguments

  • → This is Sofia's error
  • → Saves time but costs Dimension 2 points immediately
  • → The evaluator's FIRST impression is a missing structural element
  • → Never recommended

Option D: Write a very long introduction and short body

  • → Inverted proportion → Body is too thin → Arguments are undeveloped
  • → Introduction is for FRAMING, not for ARGUING
  • → If your introduction is 100 words and your body is 80 words → Structural failure
  • → Never recommended

✅ WHAT SHOULD DO

1. Memorize the Blueprint as a Physical Skeleton

SKELETON TO INTERNALIZE:

INTRODUCTION
├── Slot A: Context (1 sentence)
├── Slot B: Problématique (1 sentence)
└── Slot C: Thesis / Opinion (1 sentence) → Chapter 3 skill

BODY
├── Unit 1: Argument 1 (3–4 sentences) → Chapter 4 skill
├── Transition (1 phrase/sentence)
└── Unit 2: Argument 2 (3–4 sentences) → Chapter 4 skill

CONCLUSION
├── Slot X: Synthesis (1–2 sentences)
└── Slot Y: Ouverture (1 sentence — optional)
  • Print this skeleton
  • Keep it visible during every practice session
  • Before writing ANYTHING, mentally fill each slot with your content
  • This becomes your 3–5 minute PLANNING phase (referenced in Chapter 1's time management awareness)

2. Use Visual Paragraph Separation

  • Your response should have visible paragraph breaks
  • Minimum: 3 paragraphs (one for each part)
  • Better: 4 paragraphs (Introduction / Argument 1 / Argument 2 / Conclusion)
  • The evaluator should be able to SEE your structure before reading a single word
VISUAL APPEARANCE OF A WELL-STRUCTURED RESPONSE:

[Paragraph 1 — Introduction]
    Context sentence. Problématique sentence. Thesis sentence.

[Paragraph 2 — Argument 1]
    Reason + connector. Elaboration. Implication.

[Paragraph 3 — Argument 2]
    Transition + Reason + connector. Elaboration. Implication.

[Paragraph 4 — Conclusion]
    Synthesis sentence. Ouverture sentence.

vs.

VISUAL APPEARANCE OF SOFIA'S RESPONSE:

[One giant block of text with no paragraph breaks]
    Everything runs together. No visible structure. Evaluator sighs.
  • Paragraph breaks are FREE points on Dimension 2
  • They cost you nothing — just start a new line
  • They signal structural awareness INSTANTLY

3. Plan Your Slots BEFORE Writing Any Full Sentences

  • During your 3–5 minute planning phase:
    • → Identify your topic and question (Chapter 2)
    • → Decide your position (Chapter 3)
    • → Choose your 2 reasons (Chapter 4)
    • → Assign each reason to a body unit
    • → Note which connectors you will use (to avoid repetition)
  • This planning can be done as rough notes on scrap paper
  • Example planning notes:
PLANNING NOTES (rough, not full sentences):

INTRO: technologies → métiers disparaissent → pas préoccupant
ARG 1: histoire → nouvelles technologies = nouveaux emplois (ex: informatique)
ARG 2: automatisation → tâches créatives → qualité de vie
CONCL: adaptation + formation nécessaire
  • These 4 lines take 2 minutes to write
  • They GUARANTEE you will not lose your structure while writing
  • They prevent the "I started writing and forgot where I was going" problem

4. Ensure Your Introduction and Conclusion Are NOT Identical

COMMON MISTAKE:

Introduction thesis: "Je suis convaincue que cette évolution est positive."
Conclusion synthesis: "Je suis convaincue que cette évolution est positive."

→ This is REPETITION, not SYNTHESIS
→ The evaluator sees: "The candidate could not rephrase their own thesis"
→ Dimension 3 (vocabulary) penalized
CORRECT APPROACH:

Introduction thesis: "Je suis fermement convaincue que cette transformation
                     représente une évolution globalement positive pour la société."

Conclusion synthesis: "En définitive, la disparition de certains métiers traditionnels
                      témoigne de la capacité de nos sociétés à évoluer
                      et à s'adapter aux mutations technologiques."

→ Same POSITION, different WORDS
→ The conclusion DISTILLS the argument — it does not photocopy the introduction
→ Dimension 3 rewarded for vocabulary variety

5. Use DIFFERENT Opening Words for Each Section

  • This prevents structural monotony and signals clear section transitions
INTRODUCTION OPENERS:
├── De nos jours, ...
├── Aujourd'hui, ...
├── Dans notre société contemporaine, ...
├── Depuis quelques années, ...
└── Il est désormais courant de constater que...

BODY UNIT 1 OPENERS:
├── Tout d'abord, ...
├── En premier lieu, ...
├── D'une part, ...
└── Le premier argument en faveur de cette position est que...

BODY UNIT 2 OPENERS:
├── Par ailleurs, ...
├── De surcroît, ...
├── En outre, ...
├── D'autre part, ...
└── Au-delà de cet aspect, ...

CONCLUSION OPENERS:
├── En définitive, ...
├── En somme, ...
├── Pour conclure, ...
├── Tout compte fait, ...
└── Au terme de cette réflexion, ...
  • These openers are SIGNPOSTS — they tell the evaluator "a new section begins here"
  • They are like chapter titles in a book — the reader knows what to expect
  • Using them = Dimension 2 (cohesion) maximized with minimal effort

❌ WHAT SHOULD NOT DO

1. Do NOT Write Without a Plan

  • Writing Section B without planning = building a house without blueprints
  • You might produce something, but it will not be structurally sound
  • Even 2 minutes of planning dramatically improves structural quality
  • Sofia had all the right materials but no blueprint → Result: a pile of bricks, not a house

2. Do NOT Put Your Thesis in the Conclusion Instead of the Introduction

  • Some writing traditions place the thesis at the END
  • French argumentative tradition places it at the END OF THE INTRODUCTION
  • The evaluator expects to know your position within the first 3 sentences
  • A thesis that only appears in the conclusion = 80% of the response has no declared direction
  • → Dimension 2 severely penalized (the reader was lost for most of the text)

3. Do NOT Introduce NEW Arguments in the Conclusion

  • The conclusion is for SYNTHESIS, not for new content
  • ❌ "En conclusion, je pense que cette évolution est positive. De plus, il faut mentionner que l'intelligence artificielle crée aussi des emplois dans le secteur médical."
  • → "L'intelligence artificielle crée des emplois dans le secteur médical" is a NEW argument
  • → It belongs in the BODY, not the conclusion
  • → Introducing it here tells the evaluator: "I forgot this point and squeezed it in at the end"
  • → Dimension 2 penalized for structural disorder

4. Do NOT Write an Introduction Longer Than Your Body

  • Introduction > Body = Inverted architecture
  • The evaluator sees: lots of framing, very little substance
  • Ideal: Introduction ≈ 30–50 words / Body ≈ 120–170 words
  • If your introduction exceeds 60 words → You are over-framing
  • Cut context to essentials → Move to your thesis faster

5. Do NOT Skip Paragraph Breaks

  • Writing your entire response as ONE paragraph = invisible structure
  • Even if your IDEAS are organized, the evaluator cannot SEE the organization
  • Paragraph breaks are VISUAL EVIDENCE of structure
  • No paragraph breaks → Evaluator assumes no structure → Dimension 2 drops
  • This is the easiest fix in all of TEF preparation → Just press "enter" / start a new line between sections

6. Do NOT Begin Your Response by Copying the Prompt

  • ❌ "Certains estiment que l'apprentissage d'une langue étrangère devrait être obligatoire. Je pense que oui."
  • → You copied the prompt's words → Evaluator sees: no reformulation ability
  • → Your introduction should REPHRASE the context, not reproduce it
  • ✅ "La question de l'enseignement des langues étrangères dès le plus jeune âge suscite un débat important."
  • → Same topic, YOUR words → Evaluator sees: linguistic autonomy

7. Do NOT Use "En conclusion" AND "Pour conclure" in the Same Conclusion

  • Pick ONE conclusion opener
  • Using two = redundancy within your shortest section
  • This mirrors the Chapter 3 rule about not combining two opinion markers in one sentence

You have only 20 minutes left (time pressure)→ Write a 2-sentence intro + 1 justification unit + 2-sentence conclusion → Minimum viable structure
The prompt is simple and your opinion is clear→ Keep introduction SHORT (2 sentences) → Maximize body development
The prompt is complex or multi-layered→ Spend slightly more time on Slot B (problématique) to frame the specific angle you will address

🚫 WHEN NOT TO USE

When NOT to Use This Exact 3-Part Structure

  • Section A of TEF Expression Écrite (letters, emails) has a DIFFERENT structure
    • → Section A uses: Salutation → Body → Polite closing → Signature
    • → Do NOT apply Section B's intro-body-conclusion to a letter format
  • If a future TEF task asks for a different format (e.g., narrative, description)
    • → Adapt structure to the task
    • → But for Section B specifically → This 3-part structure is ALWAYS correct

When NOT to Use the "Ouverture" in the Conclusion

  • If you are running low on time → Skip Slot Y (ouverture) → Slot X (synthesis) is sufficient
  • If your ouverture feels forced or disconnected from the topic → Better to end cleanly with synthesis alone
  • A bad ouverture is worse than no ouverture
  • The ouverture is a BONUS, not a requirement

⚙️ HOW IT ONLY WORKS (INTERNAL MECHANICS)

Why the 3-Part Structure Exists Cognitively

HUMAN READING COGNITION:
│
├── When we encounter a text, our brain seeks 3 things:
│   ├── ORIENTATION → "What is this about? Where is this going?"
│   │   └── The INTRODUCTION satisfies this need
│   ├── SUBSTANCE → "What are the arguments? What is the evidence?"
│   │   └── The BODY satisfies this need
│   └── CLOSURE → "What is the final answer? How does this end?"
│       └── The CONCLUSION satisfies this need
│
├── If ORIENTATION is missing:
│   ├── The reader feels dropped into the middle of something
│   ├── Cognitive load increases → Reader must figure out the topic AND the argument simultaneously
│   └── This is Sofia's problem — her response started mid-argument
│
├── If SUBSTANCE is missing:
│   ├── The reader feels there is a frame but nothing inside
│   └── All setup, no payoff → Frustrating and unconvincing
│
└── If CLOSURE is missing:
    ├── The reader feels the text just... stops
    ├── No sense of completion → The argument feels abandoned
    └── The evaluator is left with an impression of incompleteness

How the Evaluator Processes Your Structure

EVALUATOR'S STRUCTURAL SCAN:
│
├── FIRST GLANCE (before reading):
│   ├── Are there visible paragraph breaks? → YES/NO
│   ├── Is the text approximately 200–250 words? → YES/NO
│   └── First impression of structural organization formed
│       (This takes the evaluator about 3 seconds)
│
├── READING THE INTRODUCTION:
│   ├── Does it contextualize the topic? → Dimension 1 check
│   ├── Does it frame the specific question? → Dimension 1 check
│   ├── Does it announce a clear position? → Dimension 1 + 2 check
│   └── If ALL three slots are present → Strong structural start
│
├── READING THE BODY:
│   ├── Are there distinct arguments? → Dimension 2 check
│   ├── Are arguments developed (not just listed)? → Dimension 2 check
│   ├── Are connectors used between and within arguments? → Dimension 2 check
│   └── Does each argument support the thesis announced in the introduction?
│       → Dimension 2 (coherence) — arguments must ALIGN with thesis
│
└── READING THE CONCLUSION:
    ├── Does it synthesize (not just repeat)? → Dimension 2 + 3 check
    ├── Does the final position match the introduction's thesis? → Dimension 2
    └── Is there a sense of closure? → Dimension 2
        → If yes → "This candidate understands French argumentative structure"
        → If no → "This candidate writes content but lacks structural awareness"

The Architecture-Content Relationship

CRITICAL INSIGHT:
│
├── Structure and content are NOT independent dimensions
├── Structure AMPLIFIES good content
├── Lack of structure DIMINISHES good content
│
├── Sofia had GOOD CONTENT but NO STRUCTURE → Score: mediocre
├── A candidate with MODERATE CONTENT but GOOD STRUCTURE → Score: decent
├── A candidate with GOOD CONTENT and GOOD STRUCTURE → Score: high
│
└── The mathematical relationship:
    Final Score ≈ Content Quality × Structure Quality
    (not Content Quality + Structure Quality)
    
    This means:
    Good content × zero structure = ZERO effective impact
    Moderate content × good structure = GOOD effective impact
    Structure is a MULTIPLIER, not an addition

How the 3 Parts Connect to Each Other Mechanically

INTRODUCTION → BODY CONNECTION:
├── The thesis (Slot C) creates a PROMISE to the reader
├── "I believe X because..." → The reader now EXPECTS to see reasons for X
├── The body FULFILLS this promise
├── If the body argues something different from the thesis → BROKEN PROMISE
│   → Evaluator sees: incoherence → Dimension 2 collapse
└── Rule: Every body argument must be traceable back to the thesis

BODY → CONCLUSION CONNECTION:
├── The body presents detailed arguments
├── The conclusion CONDENSES these arguments into a final statement
├── The conclusion should feel like the NATURAL ENDPOINT of the body
├── If the conclusion introduces ideas not in the body → DISCONNECTION
│   → Evaluator sees: new content in wrong section → Structural error
└── Rule: The conclusion contains NOTHING that was not already argued in the body

INTRODUCTION → CONCLUSION CONNECTION:
├── The introduction OPENS a question
├── The conclusion CLOSES that question
├── They form BOOKENDS around the body
├── The conclusion's position should MATCH the introduction's position
│   (Unless you deliberately changed your mind — which is rare and risky)
├── If they match → The response feels COMPLETE
└── If they contradict → The response feels CONFUSED

🕳️ COMMON PITFALLS AND FAILURE MODES

Pitfall 1: The "No Introduction" Syndrome (Sofia's Error)

  • What happens: Candidate jumps directly into arguments without framing
  • Why it happens: Anxiety about word count → Candidate wants to "get to the point"
  • How to prevent: Remember: Introduction = 30–50 words → This is NOT a major word investment → It is a minimal cost for maximum structural benefit

Pitfall 2: The "All Introduction" Syndrome

  • What happens: Candidate writes a 100-word introduction with extensive context but barely develops the body
  • Why it happens: Candidate feels comfortable paraphrasing the prompt but uncomfortable constructing arguments
  • How to prevent: Hard rule → Introduction ≤ 50 words → If you exceed this, you are over-framing

Pitfall 3: The "Copy-Paste" Conclusion

  • What happens: Conclusion is identical wording to the introduction thesis
  • Why it happens: Candidate does not know how to synthesize differently
  • How to prevent: Use a DIFFERENT opinion marker (Chapter 3) and DIFFERENT vocabulary → Same meaning, new words
  • Practice: Write your introduction thesis → Close your eyes → Write the same idea using completely different words → That is your conclusion synthesis

Pitfall 4: The "Invisible Structure" Problem

  • What happens: The response IS structured in the candidate's mind but has no paragraph breaks, no section openers, no visual markers
  • Why it happens: Candidate focuses on content and forgets that the evaluator cannot read their mind
  • How to prevent:
    • → Paragraph break between each section (minimum 3 paragraphs)
    • → Section-appropriate openers ("De nos jours" for intro / "Tout d'abord" for body / "En définitive" for conclusion)
    • → Structure must be VISIBLE, not just internal

Pitfall 5: The "Drifting Body" Problem

  • What happens: Body arguments start aligned with the thesis but gradually drift to a different topic
  • Why it happens: While writing, new ideas emerge and the candidate follows them without checking alignment
  • How to prevent: After writing each justification unit, re-read your thesis → Ask: "Does this argument SUPPORT my thesis?" → If it drifted → Adjust

Pitfall 6: The "Abrupt Ending"

  • What happens: The response ends after the last body argument with no conclusion at all
  • Why it happens: Time pressure → Candidate ran out of time → No conclusion written
  • How to prevent:
    • → Budget time: Reserve 3–4 minutes for the conclusion
    • → Emergency protocol: If you have only 1 minute left → Write ONE synthesis sentence
    • → Even "En définitive, cette évolution me paraît positive malgré certaines réserves." (one sentence) is infinitely better than nothing
    • → A one-sentence conclusion = minimal structure maintained
    • → No conclusion at all = visible structural failure

├── Body arguments must ALL trace back to the thesis
