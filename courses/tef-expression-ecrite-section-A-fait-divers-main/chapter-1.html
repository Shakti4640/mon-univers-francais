Chapter 1: What Is a Fait Divers? — Meeting the Beast

📦 CHAPTER OVERVIEW

Core Concept: Genre definition → journalistic tone → 80-word minimum → neutral voice

Phase: 1 — Very Easy (Foundation Thinking)

Dependency: None (Starting Point)

🎬 THE HYPOTHETICAL PROBLEM

You open your TEF booklet and see a single sentence printed on the page: « Un couple s'est marié au sommet d'un volcan. » Next to it, the instructions ask for at least 80 words.

You freeze. Is this a short story? An email? A news report? A blog entry? Are you supposed to invent a narrator, add dialogue, describe feelings? You don't even know what kind of text you are being asked to produce — and every second of confusion burns exam time you cannot get back.

Worse still, you suspect that even if you write fluent, correct French, choosing the wrong genre will cost you the majority of the points available for this task. The examiner isn't only grading your grammar — they are checking whether you can recognize and respect a very specific French journalistic format.

🧭 HOW TO APPROACH IT

Before you write a single word, you must name the beast. A fait divers is a short news item — the kind of filler story that sits in the corner of a French newspaper, next to the weather and the cinema listings.

It reports an unusual but real-sounding event in an impersonal, factual voice. It is not a diary entry, not a letter, not an opinion piece, not a creative story. The writer stays invisible; only the event is on stage.

The job is to extend the prompt with new, plausible facts — who exactly was involved, where precisely, when, how, and what followed. You are an imaginary journalist filing a 100-word article under deadline pressure.

🛠️ HOW TO SOLVE IT

Lock three anchors in your head before you write: (1) you are a journalist, not a character in the story; (2) you report new facts that logically extend the prompt without contradicting it; (3) you aim for 90 to 110 words — never fewer than 80, never more than about 130.

Keep your vocabulary neutral, your tenses mostly past (passé composé + imparfait), and your structure journalistic: opening with the core facts, middle with how it happened, ending with the aftermath.

If at any moment during writing you catch yourself adding an opinion, a dialogue, or a personal reaction — stop, delete it, and rephrase as a third-person fact.

✅ WHAT YOU SHOULD DO

  • Treat the prompt sentence as a headline the reader has already seen — do not rewrite it as your opening.
  • Stay strictly in the third person, using il, elle, ils, elles, on.
  • Imagine the text appearing in Le Monde, 20 Minutes, or Ouest-France — mimic that neutral register.
  • Aim for a tight paragraph of 5 to 7 sentences; this usually lands naturally in the 90–110 word zone.
  • Add at least one source attribution (selon la police, d'après les pompiers) to reinforce the news-report feel.

❌ WHAT YOU SHOULD NOT DO

  • Do not copy-paste the prompt as your opening line — rephrase with added precision.
  • Do not write « Je pense que… » or « À mon avis… » — no opinions are allowed.
  • Do not invent a dialogue scene; direct speech between characters belongs to fiction, not fait divers.
  • Do not use a friendly or emotional register (incroyable ! / malheureusement…).
  • Do not finish the text with a moral lesson or a call to reflection.

⚠️ COMMON PITFALLS & FAILURE MODES

  • Writing what is essentially a personal letter (Cher Marc, tu ne vas pas croire ce qui s'est passé…) — an instant genre failure that often caps the score at A2.
  • Padding with filler sentences (C'est vraiment incroyable ! On n'imagine pas une chose pareille…) to hit the word count — examiners detect this immediately.
  • Starting well in the right genre but slipping halfway into a short story, adding imagined thoughts of the characters.
  • Using too many adjectives of emotion (terrifiant, magnifique, effrayant) instead of factual ones (grave, léger, important).
  • Forgetting the aftermath sentence and leaving the reader with an unfinished story.

✍️ WRITING SAMPLES (3)

Sample 1 — Volcano wedding (quirky event)

Un couple britannique s'est marié au sommet d'un volcan actif, en Islande, samedi après-midi. Les mariés, accompagnés d'une dizaine d'invités, ont échangé leurs vœux à seulement quarante mètres du cratère fumant. La cérémonie, organisée par une agence spécialisée, a duré moins de trente minutes pour des raisons de sécurité. Des guides et un médecin étaient présents en cas d'évacuation. Selon les organisateurs, l'activité volcanique avait été jugée stable la veille. Aucun incident n'a été signalé. Le couple a déclaré vouloir « une union mémorable ».

Sample 2 — House fire (serious news)

Un incendie s'est déclaré dans un immeuble du centre de Bordeaux, jeudi soir vers 22 h. Les flammes, parties d'un appartement du deuxième étage, se sont rapidement propagées à l'étage supérieur. Les pompiers, arrivés sur place en moins de dix minutes, ont évacué une vingtaine de résidents. Deux personnes ont été légèrement intoxiquées par la fumée et transportées à l'hôpital par précaution. Le feu a été maîtrisé vers minuit. Selon les premiers éléments, l'origine du sinistre serait électrique. Une enquête a été ouverte.

Sample 3 — Lucky rescue (positive insolite)

Un randonneur allemand de 34 ans a été secouru sain et sauf dans les Pyrénées, dimanche après-midi, après avoir passé près de vingt heures bloqué sur une corniche rocheuse. L'homme, parti seul samedi matin, avait glissé en tentant un raccourci. Il a pu alerter les secours grâce à son téléphone portable, dont la batterie est tombée peu après. Un hélicoptère de la gendarmerie l'a localisé grâce à sa dernière position GPS. Le randonneur, légèrement hypothermique, a été hospitalisé. Il est hors de danger.

🎓 DEEP DIVE — Anatomy of the Genre — What French Newsrooms Actually Mean by « Fait Divers »

The French press tradition gives the fait divers a very specific slot. It sits in the rubrique « faits divers » of a regional or national daily — typically a short, self-contained 100-word item reporting an unusual but real-sounding event, written in an impersonal voice. Think of it as the written equivalent of a 30-second radio news flash: one event, five facts, zero opinion.

Three markers signal the genre to an examiner within the first sentence: a past-tense verb of occurrence (s'est produit, a eu lieu, s'est déclaré), an anchor of time and place (hier soir, vers 22 h, à Bordeaux), and a third-person subject that is either a role (un conducteur, une passante) or an event (un incendie, un cambriolage). Miss all three and the reader hears an email; miss two and they hear a diary.

The invisible contract with the reader is equally precise. The fait divers answers the five Ws in the first two sentences, develops the how and why in the middle, and closes with the aftermath — investigation, hospital, arrest, appeal to witnesses. Anything outside that arc (feelings, moral lessons, dialogue, personal reactions) breaks the contract and costs points.

A practical mental test: read your draft aloud and ask whether it could appear, unchanged, in 20 Minutes next to real news. If yes, you are in the genre. If your text would need an introduction (« Je vais vous raconter ce qui s'est passé… ») or a conclusion (« On voit bien que… »), you have drifted.

On the TEF, the single most common genre failure is the polite narrative: the candidate tells the story correctly but uses the voice of a storyteller rather than a journalist. The cure is surgical — strip every je, every adverb of emotion (malheureusement, heureusement, incroyablement), every sentence that does not add a concrete fact.
